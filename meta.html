Multilevel Meta-analysis

Table of Contents

    Background

    Fisheries managers faced a fragmented evidence base: 23 peer-reviewed studies on angler satisfaction existed, but they used different measures, reported results inconsistently, and often pointed in conflicting directions. Without a synthesis, it was impossible to know which factors reliably shaped satisfaction or to base policy on more than isolated findings.

    I conducted a multilevel meta-analysis synthesizing 23 studies (33 datasets, 172 effect sizes) to address this challenge. The multilevel structure accounted for statistical dependencies—multiple effect sizes nested within datasets, nested within studies—ensuring unbiased estimates when individual studies contributed multiple results. I also conducted publication bias diagnostics (funnel plots, Begg-Mazumdar tests, sensitivity analyses) to confirm the robustness of findings.

    To make results accessible to stakeholders, I created forest plots and meta-regression visualizations designed for policymakers—translating complex statistical outputs into clear, actionable guidance. The outcome was a coherent, decision-ready framework that distilled scattered research into evidence-based priorities managers could trust when designing policies and programs: which factors have the strongest effects on satisfaction, how context moderates these relationships, and where to invest limited resources for maximum impact.

    Executive Summary

    Across 23 studies on recreational angler satisfaction, results were highly fragmented: different measures, inconsistent reporting, and sometimes contradictory conclusions. By applying a multilevel meta-analysis, we synthesized this body of work into a clear, evidence-based picture of what drives satisfaction across settings.

    The analysis revealed that catch-related components—catch success, harvest, and fish size—consistently show the strongest positive effects (effect sizes >0.4) on satisfaction. Two non-catch factors, space and congestion, also emerged as significant and influential, while relaxation, facilities, aesthetics, and mastery showed moderate but reliable relationships. By contrast, social quality and water quality perceptions were not consistently significant.

    Moderator analyses highlighted that species, country, and satisfaction metric shape how strongly different components matter. For example, aesthetics, mastery, and relaxation are especially important to freshwater anglers and in German studies, while catch-related factors remain universally strong across all contexts. Model diagnostics confirmed no evidence of publication bias, with symmetric funnel plots and robust sensitivity checks supporting the reliability of these findings.

    Taken together, these results provide managers and policymakers with clear, defensible insights: investments that improve catch outcomes and reduce congestion will yield the largest gains in satisfaction, while the importance of non-catch aspects depends on context. The approach also demonstrates the power of meta-analysis to turn fragmented evidence into decision-ready guidance—a transferable method for other contested or complex management domains.

    Key Findings: What the Multilevel Model Reveals

    The findings below emerge from a multilevel meta-analysis of 23 studies (33 datasets, 172 effect sizes) on angler satisfaction. The three-level model accounts for statistical dependencies (effect sizes nested within datasets nested within studies), producing unbiased pooled estimates and enabling context-specific predictions through meta-regression. All visualizations are designed for policymakers and fisheries managers, translating complex statistical outputs into clear, actionable guidance.

    Forest Plot: Which Components Drive Satisfaction?

    To understand which components of an angling experience mattered most, we estimated pooled effect sizes for 11 experience components against overall satisfaction across the 23 studies. The forest plot below shows the effect magnitude for each factor, with confidence intervals indicating precision. This visualization format is standard in meta-analysis and allows managers to quickly identify high-leverage intervention points.

    Forest plot showing pooled effect sizes (Fisher's z transformed) for 11 satisfaction components. Strong effects (g>0.4) are shown in dark blue, moderate effects (g>0.2) in light blue, and non-significant effects (95% CI includes zero) in grey. Horizontal bars represent 95% confidence intervals; narrower bars indicate more precise estimates.

    Key Patterns from the Multilevel Model:

    • Catch-related factors dominated (effect sizes > 0.4): Catch success, harvest, and fish size consistently showed the strongest positive effects on overall satisfaction across all 23 studies—these are the highest-leverage variables for improvement. The multilevel model confirmed these effects remain robust even after accounting for dependencies between multiple results from the same study.
    • Space and congestion matter too (g > 0.3): Anglers valued room on the water and disliked crowding; both emerged as strong non-catch determinants with effect sizes comparable to biological factors. This finding held across freshwater and saltwater contexts, suggesting capacity management is universally important.
    • Moderate but meaningful effects (0.2 < g < 0.4): Relaxation, aesthetics, facilities, and mastery all showed moderate yet significant relationships with satisfaction. While not as strong as catch factors, these components still represent viable intervention points, especially in contexts where catch rates are difficult to improve.
    • No consistent effect (CI includes zero): Social quality and perceived water quality were not significantly related to satisfaction across studies—confidence intervals included zero. This doesn't mean they never matter, but rather that their effects are inconsistent or context-dependent and cannot be relied upon as universal drivers.

    📊 Reading the Forest Plot

    Each row represents one satisfaction component pooled across all studies that measured it. The dot shows the estimated effect size. The horizontal bar shows the 95% confidence interval—if it crosses the vertical zero line, the effect is not statistically significant.

    Practical interpretation: Components with effect sizes > 0.4 (dark blue) should be priority targets for policy interventions. Components with effect sizes 0.2-0.4 (light blue) are secondary priorities. Components in grey (non-significant) may still matter in specific contexts but lack consistent evidence across studies.

    Meta-Regression: How Context Shapes What Matters

    One advantage of multilevel meta-analysis is the ability to test moderators—variables that explain why effect sizes differ across studies. We used meta-regression to separate results by key subgroups (country, species, satisfaction metric), revealing how context shifts the importance of different experience dimensions. This allows managers to filter evidence by contexts most relevant to their fishery, rather than relying on one-size-fits-all recommendations.

    Meta-regression results showing how effect sizes vary by country, target species, and satisfaction metric. Each panel displays pooled estimates with 95% confidence intervals. Differences between groups are considered significant when confidence bars do not overlap. Grey bars indicate non-significant effects within that subgroup.

    Context-Specific Patterns:

    • Catch-related factors remained strong across all contexts: Regardless of country, species, or satisfaction metric, catch success, harvest, and fish size consistently showed large positive effects (g > 0.4). This makes them universally reliable intervention points.
    • Aesthetics, mastery, and relaxation stronger in Germany and freshwater contexts: German studies and freshwater fisheries showed significantly larger effect sizes for these non-catch components compared to U.S. studies and saltwater fisheries. This suggests cultural and ecological contexts shape what anglers value beyond catch.
    • Annual satisfaction metrics amplify non-catch effects: When satisfaction was measured over a full season (rather than trip-by-trip), aesthetics, facilities, and social quality showed stronger effects. This implies that long-term satisfaction depends more on the "whole experience" than individual trip outcomes.
    • Congestion effects universal: Space and crowding concerns showed consistent negative effects across all subgroups tested, reinforcing that capacity management is a cross-cutting priority.

    For managers, the key takeaway is that the multilevel meta-analysis provides context-specific predictions rather than one-size-fits-all recommendations. By filtering results to match local conditions (e.g., freshwater charter fishery in the U.S.), managers can identify which levers are most likely to improve satisfaction in their setting—whether that means emphasizing catch, investing in facilities, or addressing congestion.

    🔄 Why meta-regression matters for policy: Standard meta-analysis gives you an "average" effect across all studies—potentially masking that catch matters more in some contexts while aesthetics matter more in others. Meta-regression reveals these moderating effects, showing policymakers which evidence applies to their specific situation. This prevents overgeneralizing findings from one context (e.g., saltwater U.S. charters) to very different contexts (e.g., German freshwater bank fishing).

    Publication Bias Diagnostics: Is the Evidence Reliable?

    Because meta-analyses combine results across many independent studies, it's important to test whether the available evidence is systematically biased—for example, if studies with non-significant or "negative" results were less likely to be published. We conducted multiple publication bias diagnostics to assess the robustness of findings.

    Funnel Plot (Visual Inspection)

    The funnel plot below shows the relationship between effect size and precision (standard error). In the absence of publication bias, the plot should be symmetric—small studies (low precision, bottom of funnel) should scatter evenly around the pooled estimate, while large studies (high precision, top of funnel) should cluster tightly. Asymmetry suggests that small studies with certain results (e.g., null findings) are missing.

    Funnel plot showing effect size (x-axis) vs. standard error (y-axis). The vertical line represents the pooled estimate. Symmetry around this line indicates lack of publication bias.

    Statistical Tests & Sensitivity Analyses:

    • Begg-Mazumdar rank correlation test: No significant asymmetry (p = .356; Kendall's τ = 0.487). This indicates that effect size and variance are not correlated—as expected in an unbiased sample.
    • Egger's regression test: Tests for small-study effects by regressing effect sizes on precision. Result: no significant intercept (p = .412), confirming lack of small-study bias.
    • Trim-and-fill analysis: Imputes potentially missing studies to assess impact on pooled estimates. Result: zero studies imputed, suggesting the current evidence base is complete.
    • Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis: Removed each study individually and recalculated pooled estimates. Result: no single study disproportionately influenced findings—effect sizes remained stable across all iterations.

    ✓ Publication Bias Conclusion

    All five diagnostics converged on the same conclusion: no evidence of substantial publication bias. The funnel plot is symmetric, statistical tests are non-significant, trim-and-fill imputes zero missing studies, and sensitivity analyses show robustness. This provides high confidence that synthesized findings reflect the true evidence base rather than selective reporting. For policymakers, this means the identified satisfaction drivers (catch, congestion, space) are reliable foundations for decision-making.

    For decision-makers, the implication is that the identified satisfaction drivers are robust across the published evidence base, rather than reflecting a skewed subset of studies. Managers can trust that investments targeting catch-related factors, congestion reduction, and space management will yield consistent returns across diverse contexts.

    Recommendations

    Priority Ranking Based on Effect Sizes

    • Elevate catch-related factors: Consistently large effect sizes mean these remain the most reliable levers for improving angler satisfaction across contexts—optimize here first.
    • Don't overlook non-catch drivers: Space and congestion also had major effects—showing that crowding can offset biological success and should be a co-equal management priority.
    • Support the "whole experience" view: Facilities, aesthetics, and relaxation carried moderate but significant influence, reminding managers that quality infrastructure and environment matter as much as fish.

    Context-Specific Management

    • Tailor by species: Freshwater anglers placed higher importance on mastery and relaxation, while catch-related aspects were equally important across species—highlighting where context shapes priorities.
    • Adapt by geography: German studies emphasized aesthetics and social experiences more than North American ones, suggesting cultural differences in what drives satisfaction.
    • Measure satisfaction carefully: Annual satisfaction scores captured stronger effects for many drivers than trip-level scores—showing that survey design choices shape insights.

    Program & Policy Applications

    • Balance biological and social strategies: Stocking and harvest management remain key, but capacity limits, site design, and aesthetics should be built into planning.
    • Invest in crowding solutions: Rotating access, spatial zoning, or timing policies can directly address congestion—a high-impact satisfaction driver.
    • Frame communication broadly: Outreach should highlight not just fish availability but also the quality of space, environment, and community around angling.

    Data & Future Research

    • Standardize measurement: Future studies should adopt common satisfaction metrics to reduce heterogeneity and strengthen comparability.
    • Expand beyond catch: More consistent reporting of non-catch dimensions (e.g., social quality, water quality) would fill key evidence gaps.
    • Continue synthesis: Updating this meta-analysis with new data ensures that management keeps pace with evolving angler values.

    Clarifying Questions, Assumptions, and Caveats

    Clarifying Questions

    • Geographic scope: Would results hold in regions not represented (e.g., Asia, South America, Africa)?
    • Alternative study designs: How might including economics-focused studies (e.g., choice models) change conclusions?
    • Interaction of factors: Do satisfaction components operate additively, or are there thresholds/synergies across dimensions?

    Assumptions

    • Inclusion criteria: Only studies that measured both component-level and overall satisfaction were included, ensuring comparability.
    • Framework choice: Adopted the “sum-of-satisfactions” approach, assuming each component contributes independently.
    • Comparability across studies: Standardized all findings to a common effect-size metric to allow pooling.

    Model Limitations

    • Linear assumptions: The model assumes additive effects; potential interactions or nonlinear relationships (e.g., threshold effects for crowding) were not tested.
    • Observational data: All underlying studies are correlational—causal claims require experimental validation.
    • Generalization bounds: Predictions are most reliable within the range of observed contexts (primarily U.S., Germany, Canada); extrapolation to underrepresented regions should be done cautiously.
