Harvest & Release Behavior — Data Analysis Project

Survey data, behavioral modeling, and reproducible reporting

Table of Contents

    Background

    Fisheries managers face a core challenge: angler behavior drives mortality as much as regulations do. Even when legal harvest rules are clear, outcomes depend on whether anglers choose to keep or release fish— a binary decision that shapes population dynamics and conservation success. In Germany, where cultural norms lean strongly toward consumption, voluntary catch-and-release remains a pivotal yet unpredictable factor. Our project tackles this problem by analyzing large-scale trip records and angler data to uncover what shapes harvest decisions, using logistic mixed-effects models to predict this binary outcome while accounting for species, angler traits, and regional context.

    By merging nearly 20,000 trip-level records with composite psychological indices built from multi-item survey scales—capturing skill, lifestyle centrality, and consumption orientation— we provide management with evidence-based levers to align conservation targets with actual on-the-water behavior.

    Executive Summary

    Voluntary catch-and-release (vCandR) is increasingly shaping the future of German fisheries. Drawing on nearly 20,000 trip-level records from anglers in two northern states (Lower Saxony in the West, Mecklenburg–Western Pomerania in the East), our study offers one of the most comprehensive looks at how culture, angler traits, and context interact to drive harvest decisions. We find clear contrasts between regions: East German anglers harvest a larger share of their catch, while West German anglers demonstrate higher release rates—reflecting legacies of culture, governance, and norms.

    Beyond geography, species, fish size, and angler characteristics strongly influence behavior. Marine and migratory species are kept more often than freshwater fish, while trout and cod show consistently high harvest rates. Age, lifestyle centrality, and consumptive orientation push anglers toward keeping fish, whereas higher skill and stronger commitment reduce harvest likelihood. Fish size plays a pivotal role: intermediate-sized fish are most likely to be harvested, while both small and trophy-sized fish are more often released.

    Taken together, these findings highlight both constraints and opportunities for managers. Harvest and release practices are not uniform but shaped by social context, cultural legacies, and species-specific norms. Recognizing this diversity can support more tailored regulations, communication, and stakeholder engagement, ensuring that management strategies align with how anglers actually behave on the water.

    Methods

    Data Sources & Structure

    We analyzed 19,958 fishing trips from 1,169 anglers across two German regions with contrasting cultural and institutional contexts: Mecklenburg–Western Pomerania (MWP, 2006-2007), where access is largely open, and Lower Saxony (LS, 2011-2012), where angling is organized through clubs. Both regions used structured diary programs where anglers recorded trip details in real time: timing, location, effort, target species, catch counts, fish sizes, and critically—whether each fish was harvested or released (the binary outcome we modeled).

    Composite Psychological Indices

    From companion surveys, we constructed composite indices capturing angler psychology and specialization using validated multi-item scales:

    • Centrality to lifestyle (CTL): 6-item scale measuring how central fishing is to personal identity and lifestyle (α = 0.84)
    • Behavioral commitment: Composite of annual trip frequency, years of experience, and equipment investment
    • Self-perceived skill: Single-item measure (1-5 scale) from novice to expert
    • Consumption orientation: 4-item scale measuring preference for harvest/consumption over catch-and-release (α = 0.78)
    • Catch orientation: Preference for quantity and size of catch vs. other trip qualities

    All multi-item scales were averaged to create composite scores. This approach transformed raw survey responses into interpretable behavioral profiles that could be linked to harvest decisions.

    import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# ============================================
# Build composite psychological indices
# ============================================
# NOTE: These are created from angler-level survey data
# This is appropriate because they're angler characteristics, not trip outcomes

# Centrality to lifestyle (6 items, α=0.84)
ctl_items = ['ctl_1', 'ctl_2', 'ctl_3', 'ctl_4', 'ctl_5', 'ctl_6']
angler_data['CTL'] = angler_data[ctl_items].mean(axis=1)

# Consumption orientation (4 items, α=0.78)
cons_items = ['cons_1', 'cons_2', 'cons_3', 'cons_4']
angler_data['consumption_orient'] = angler_data[cons_items].mean(axis=1)

# Behavioral commitment (composite measure)
angler_data['commitment'] = (
    angler_data['annual_trips'] * 0.4 + 
    angler_data['years_experience'] * 0.3 + 
    angler_data['equipment_investment'] * 0.3
)

# Self-perceived skill (already single item, 1-5 scale)
# angler_data['skill'] already exists

# Merge angler-level indices with trip-level data
trip_data = trip_data.merge(
    angler_data[['angler_id', 'CTL', 'skill', 'age', 'consumption_orient', 'commitment']], 
    on='angler_id'
)

print(f"Created indices for {len(angler_data)} anglers")
print(f"Merged with {len(trip_data)} trip records")

    Trip Context Variables

    Beyond angler traits, we captured trip-level context:

    • Catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE): Standardized within species to account for different catch rate expectations
    • Fish size: Individual length measurements for size-selectivity analysis
    • Target species: Primary species sought on each trip
    • Season, region, and other trip characteristics

    Analytical Approach: Logistic Mixed-Effects Models

    We used logistic mixed-effects regression to predict the binary harvest/release decision for each fish caught. This approach accounts for the hierarchical structure of the data—multiple fish within trips, and multiple trips within anglers—while modeling how angler characteristics, species identity, fish size, and regional context influence harvest probability.

    Key modeling features:

    • Binary outcome: Harvest (1) vs. Release (0) for each individual fish
    • Fixed effects: Angler attributes (age, skill, CTL, consumption orientation), species, fish size, CPUE, region, and their interactions
    • Random effects: Angler-level and trip-level random intercepts to account for individual harvest tendencies and trip-specific conditions not captured by measured predictors
    • Species-specific models: Separate analyses for major species (pike, perch, carp, trout, cod, zander) to capture species-specific size-selectivity curves
    • Pipeline structure: Preprocessing (scaling, encoding) applied using sklearn pipelines to prevent data leakage—transformations fit only on training data, then applied to test data

    Below is a simplified implementation using sklearn's LogisticRegression with proper pipeline architecture to prevent data leakage, which demonstrates the core modeling approach. Note that the full analysis used mixed-effects models (via statsmodels or R's lme4) to properly account for the nested data structure (fish → trips → anglers), which sklearn's standard logistic regression does not support.

    💻 View Complete Model Pipeline Code 
    import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report, roc_auc_score, confusion_matrix
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import joblib

# ============================================
# STEP 1: Prepare features and target
# ============================================

# Define feature columns
numeric_features = ['age', 'skill', 'CTL', 'consumption_orient', 'commitment',
                    'cpue_std', 'fish_length_cm']
categorical_features = ['species', 'region', 'season']

X = trip_data[numeric_features + categorical_features].copy()
y = trip_data['harvested']  # Binary: 1 = harvested, 0 = released

# Handle missing values
X = X.fillna(X.median(numeric_only=True))

print(f"Dataset: {len(X)} fish across {trip_data['angler_id'].nunique()} anglers")
print(f"Harvest rate: {y.mean():.1%}")

# ============================================
# STEP 2: Train-test split BEFORE transformations
# ============================================
# CRITICAL: Split data BEFORE scaling to prevent data leakage

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=42, stratify=y
)

print(f"\nTraining set: {len(X_train)} samples ({y_train.mean():.1%} harvested)")
print(f"Test set: {len(X_test)} samples ({y_test.mean():.1%} harvested)")

# ============================================
# STEP 3: Build preprocessing pipeline
# ============================================
# Ensures scaling is fit ONLY on training data

preprocessor = ColumnTransformer(
    transformers=[
        ('num', StandardScaler(), numeric_features),
        ('cat', OneHotEncoder(drop='first', sparse_output=False), categorical_features)
    ],
    remainder='passthrough'
)

# Build full pipeline
pipeline = Pipeline(steps=[
    ('preprocessor', preprocessor),
    ('classifier', LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000, random_state=42, 
                                     class_weight='balanced'))
])

# ============================================
# STEP 4: Fit pipeline on training data
# ============================================

pipeline.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("\n✓ Pipeline fitted on training data")

# ============================================
# STEP 5: Evaluate model performance
# ============================================

# Training performance
y_train_pred = pipeline.predict(X_train)
y_train_proba = pipeline.predict_proba(X_train)[:, 1]
train_acc = pipeline.score(X_train, y_train)
train_auc = roc_auc_score(y_train, y_train_proba)

# Test performance (using training scaling parameters)
y_test_pred = pipeline.predict(X_test)
y_test_proba = pipeline.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
test_acc = pipeline.score(X_test, y_test)
test_auc = roc_auc_score(y_test, y_test_proba)

print(f"\n{'='*60}")
print(f"PERFORMANCE METRICS")
print(f"{'='*60}")
print(f"Train Accuracy: {train_acc:.3f} | Train AUC: {train_auc:.3f}")
print(f"Test Accuracy:  {test_acc:.3f}  | Test AUC:  {test_auc:.3f}")
print(f"{'='*60}")

# Confusion matrix
print("\nTest Set Confusion Matrix:")
cm = confusion_matrix(y_test, y_test_pred)
print(f"                Predicted")
print(f"              Released  Harvested")
print(f"Actual Released    {cm[0,0]:5d}     {cm[0,1]:5d}")
print(f"       Harvested   {cm[1,0]:5d}     {cm[1,1]:5d}")

# Classification report
print("\nTest Set Classification Report:")
print(classification_report(y_test, y_test_pred, 
                          target_names=['Released', 'Harvested']))

# Cross-validation
cv_scores = cross_val_score(pipeline, X_train, y_train, cv=5, scoring='roc_auc')
print(f"5-Fold CV AUC: {cv_scores.mean():.3f} (+/- {cv_scores.std():.3f})")

# ============================================
# STEP 6: Examine feature importance
# ============================================

# Extract feature names after preprocessing
feature_names = (
    numeric_features + 
    list(pipeline.named_steps['preprocessor']
         .named_transformers_['cat']
         .get_feature_names_out(categorical_features))
)

# Extract coefficients
coefficients = pipeline.named_steps['classifier'].coef_[0]

coef_df = pd.DataFrame({
    'feature': feature_names,
    'coefficient': coefficients,
    'abs_coef': np.abs(coefficients)
}).sort_values('abs_coef', ascending=False)

print(f"\n{'='*60}")
print("TOP 10 PREDICTORS OF HARVEST")
print(f"{'='*60}")
for idx, row in coef_df.head(10).iterrows():
    print(f"{row['feature']:30s} {row['coefficient']:7.3f}")
print(f"{'='*60}")

# ============================================
# STEP 7: Generate predictions for new fish
# ============================================

# Example: 55cm Pike caught by experienced angler
sample_fish = pd.DataFrame({
    'age': [65],
    'skill': [4],
    'CTL': [5.2],
    'consumption_orient': [4.5],
    'commitment': [150],
    'cpue_std': [0.3],
    'fish_length_cm': [55],
    'species': ['Pike'],
    'region': ['LS'],
    'season': ['Summer']
})

harvest_prob = pipeline.predict_proba(sample_fish)[:, 1][0]
prediction = pipeline.predict(sample_fish)[0]

print(f"\n{'='*60}")
print(f"PREDICTION: 55cm Pike (Experienced Angler, Summer)")
print(f"{'='*60}")
print(f"Harvest probability: {harvest_prob:.1%}")
print(f"Predicted outcome: {'HARVESTED' if prediction == 1 else 'RELEASED'}")
print(f"{'='*60}")

# ============================================
# STEP 8: Size-selectivity curve
# ============================================

# Generate predictions across size range
size_range = pd.DataFrame({
    'fish_length_cm': np.arange(30, 101, 1),
    'age': [50] * 71,
    'skill': [3] * 71,
    'CTL': [3.5] * 71,
    'consumption_orient': [3.0] * 71,
    'commitment': [100] * 71,
    'cpue_std': [0.5] * 71,
    'species': ['Pike'] * 71,
    'region': ['LS'] * 71,
    'season': ['Summer'] * 71
})

size_range['harvest_prob'] = pipeline.predict_proba(size_range)[:, 1]

# Visualize
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 6))
ax.plot(size_range['fish_length_cm'], size_range['harvest_prob'], 
        linewidth=2.5, color='steelblue', label='Predicted harvest probability')
ax.axvline(x=45, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=2, 
          label='Legal minimum size (45 cm)')
ax.axhline(y=0.5, color='gray', linestyle=':', alpha=0.5)
ax.set_xlabel('Fish Length (cm)', fontsize=12, fontweight='bold')
ax.set_ylabel('Predicted Harvest Probability', fontsize=12, fontweight='bold')
ax.set_title('Size-Selectivity Curve: Pike (Lower Saxony, Summer)', 
            fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')
ax.legend(fontsize=10, loc='best')
ax.grid(alpha=0.3, linestyle='--')
ax.set_ylim(0, 1)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

# ============================================
# STEP 9: Save pipeline for deployment
# ============================================

joblib.dump(pipeline, 'harvest_model_pipeline.pkl')
print("\n✓ Pipeline saved to 'harvest_model_pipeline.pkl'")
print("✓ Load later: pipeline = joblib.load('harvest_model_pipeline.pkl')")

    Insights: Deep Dive

    💡 Bottom Line: What Predicts Harvest Decisions

    Using logistic mixed-effects models on nearly 20,000 fish-level decisions (binary outcome: harvest vs. release), we identified the strongest predictors of whether anglers keep or release their catch:

    • Consumption orientation (composite index from 4-item scale): Strongest predictor—high-consumption anglers harvest at 2× the rate of low-consumption anglers
    • Fish size: Intermediate-sized fish (just above legal minimums) most likely to be harvested; trophy fish increasingly released
    • Regional culture: East German anglers harvest 15-20% more than West German anglers at identical catch conditions
    • Species identity: Trout and cod harvested at 3-4× the rate of pike, regardless of angler characteristics

    Why this matters: Harvest mortality isn't just about regulations—it's driven by psychology, culture, and species-specific norms. One-size-fits-all management strategies miss these critical drivers.

    🔬 Modeling Approach

    Method: Logistic mixed-effects regression predicting binary outcome (harvest = 1, release = 0) for each fish
    Sample: 19,958 fish across 1,169 anglers (nested structure: fish → trips → anglers)
    Key Features: Composite psychological indices (CTL from 6 items, consumption orientation from 4 items), fish size, species, CPUE, region
    Random Effects: Angler-level and trip-level intercepts to account for individual harvest tendencies
    Why mixed-effects: Standard logistic regression ignores the nested structure—same angler makes multiple decisions, violating independence assumptions

    Species-Specific Harvest Patterns Across Regions

    These curves show predicted harvest probability from the logistic mixed-effects model across standardized catch rates (CPUE). The model reveals how species identity and regional culture interact— identical catch conditions produce vastly different harvest outcomes.

    What the Model Reveals:

    • Cultural baseline (East vs. West): Mecklenburg–Western Pomerania (MWP, East Germany) anglers consistently harvested 15-20% more fish than Lower Saxony (LS, West Germany) anglers, even after controlling for all angler characteristics and trip context. This persistent gap reflects lasting cultural legacies from different governance and resource access histories.
    • Catch scarcity effect: Across both regions, harvest probability rises when CPUE is low— anglers are more likely to keep fish when they're harder to come by. This "scarcity response" suggests psychological drivers beyond simple preference: when fish are scarce, perceived value increases.
    • Species differences (3-4× variation): Trout and cod show persistently high harvest probabilities (60-80% across catch rates), while pike shows the opposite pattern (20-40% harvest). This isn't explained by size or catch rates—it's species-specific cultural norms about what's "worth keeping."
    • Regional × species interactions: The East-West gap varies by species—MWP anglers harvest more carp but fewer perch, cod, and zander relative to LS anglers. This nuance shows that cultural effects aren't uniform but shaped by local traditions around specific species.
    🔄 Transferable insight: Customer behavior varies systematically by segment (region = market segment here). A "one-size-fits-all" product strategy would miss these 15-20% baseline differences—just as fisheries management can't apply uniform harvest expectations across cultural contexts.

    How Composite Psychological Indices Predict Harvest Behavior

    By constructing composite indices from multi-item survey scales and standardizing them (z-scores), the model isolates how each psychological trait shifts harvest probability. These curves show the partial effects of six angler attributes, holding all other factors constant.

    Composite Index Effects:

    • Consumption orientation (4-item scale, α=0.78): The strongest predictor in the model. Anglers scoring high on consumption orientation (values harvest, food use) harvest at 2× the rate of low-consumption anglers. Going from -1 SD to +1 SD on this index shifts harvest probability from ~40% to ~80% for many species. This demonstrates how multi-item psychological scales outperform single-item demographics.
    • Centrality to lifestyle (6-item scale, α=0.84): Anglers for whom fishing is central to identity harvest more frequently. The effect is particularly pronounced in Lower Saxony (LS), suggesting cultural reinforcement— in club-based systems, identity-driven harvest may be socially rewarded.
    • Skill (single-item, 1-5 scale): Higher self-perceived skill reduces harvest rates. Experienced anglers are more selective, more likely to release fish—especially for species like pike and cyprinids. This pattern holds even after controlling for commitment and lifestyle centrality.
    • Behavioral commitment (composite: trips × experience × investment): Similar to skill—higher commitment anglers harvest less. They're invested in long-term fishery health, not just immediate consumption.
    • Age (continuous): Older anglers consistently harvest more, likely reflecting generational norms around consumption vs. catch-and-release. This demographic effect is modest but persistent.
    • Catch orientation (preference for catching vs. keeping): Showed no significant effect once consumption orientation was included—suggesting that specific values (consumption) predict behavior better than broad stated preferences (catch orientation). Lesson: Measure specific attitudes, not generic preferences.

    🎯 Why composite indices matter: Single-item measures (age, skill) have modest effects. But multi-item psychological indices (CTL from 6 items, consumption from 4 items) show 2× the predictive power. Feature engineering—transforming raw survey responses into validated composite scores—turns weak signals into strong predictors. This same principle applies to customer segmentation, churn prediction, or any domain where psychology drives behavior.

    Size-Selectivity Curves: Where Anglers Choose to Release

    Separate logistic models for each species link individual fish size to harvest probability. Pairing predicted curves with observed size distributions reveals where regulations align with behavior— and where angler selectivity adds additional conservation value.

    Size-Selectivity Patterns:

    • Intermediate-size peak: Harvest probability rises steeply with fish length, peaking just above legal minimum-size limits (marked by dashed vertical lines). Fish at 110-120% of the minimum size are most likely to be harvested—they're legal, desirable size for consumption, and not yet "trophy" status.
    • Trophy release effect (for some species): Beyond intermediate sizes, harvest probability declines for carp and pike—anglers voluntarily release large individuals. This "trophy ethic" provides conservation benefit by protecting broodstock, even without regulations requiring it. For trout and perch, no such effect exists— they're harvested at high rates regardless of size.
    • Species-specific curves: Trout and perch show flat, high harvest rates across all sizes (60-80%), reflecting strong consumption preferences. Pike shows the opposite—declining harvest with size, driven by catch-and-release norms in the angling community.
    • Regulatory alignment: Inflection points in the curves coincide closely with statutory size limits, confirming that angler choices and legal thresholds move in tandem. Regulations shape expectations, which in turn reinforce compliance.
    • Robustness check: Sensitivity analysis (raising all limits by 20%) confirmed the size effect persists— harvest behavior is genuinely size-driven, not an artifact of current regulations. The pattern would hold even if limits changed.
    🔄 Transferable insight: Customer behavior often shows non-linear patterns—e.g., moderate-price products convert best (too cheap = low quality signal, too expensive = sticker shock). Size-selectivity curves here show the same principle: intermediate fish are kept, very small and very large fish are released. Identifying these thresholds requires modeling non-linear relationships, not just linear regressions.

    Recommendations

    Communication & Outreach

    • Frame species differently: Position marine and trout harvest as culturally appropriate, while promoting stewardship narratives for pike and carp where voluntary release is already common.
    • Highlight trophy release norms: Share stories of anglers releasing large pike/carp to build social approval for protecting broodstock.
    • Segment messages by angler type: Skilled and highly avid anglers can be recruited as peer role models; centrality-driven anglers respond better to “protecting our waters” framing.

    Policy & Regulation

    • Adopt size-based protections: Slot or trophy limits can reinforce existing behavior where anglers already release the biggest fish.
    • Tailor rules regionally: In MWP, emphasize daily limits and food-value guidance; in LS, clubs can trial selective-harvest policies with peer enforcement.
    • Link regulation to behavior: Align legal thresholds with natural inflection points in harvest probability to improve compliance and outcomes.

    Monitoring & Adaptation

    • Standardize angler logs: Expand diary/logbook systems for trip-level harvest/release tracking across regions.
    • Pilot test changes: Trial new size or slot limits for 1–2 years with pre-set KPIs to evaluate effectiveness.
    • Track culture shifts: Use shorter pulse surveys to detect generational changes in release norms more quickly than long-interval studies.

    Clarifying Questions, Assumptions, Caveats

    Clarifying Questions

    • Culture vs. time: How much of the East–West difference reflects lasting cultural traditions versus shifts over time in release behavior?
    • Voluntary vs. regulatory: To what extent were recorded releases driven by personal choice versus club or state rules?
    • Generational change: Are younger anglers systematically more release-oriented, or are differences mainly regional?

    Assumptions

    • Representativeness: Diary participants are assumed to reflect the wider angler populations in each state, despite different recruitment methods (phone vs. club-based).
    • Regulation baseline: Analyses assume anglers followed state minimum-size rules, even though some clubs may have stricter requirements.
    • Stable predictors: Specialization, skill, and consumptive orientation are treated as consistent traits, not shifting within the year of study.

    Caveats

    • Timing mismatch: The two surveys were conducted a decade apart, so regional contrasts may partly reflect temporal change rather than pure cultural difference.
    • Self-reporting: Data rely on anglers’ diaries, which may under- or overstate behaviors due to recall or social desirability bias.
    • Interpretation limits: Models capture associations, not causation; some drivers of voluntary release remain uncertain.
