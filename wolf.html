Wolf Tolerance Survey — Data Science Case Study

Featured project: longitudinal survey data · multi-language workflow (Python · SQL · R)

Table of Contents

    Background

    Montana Fish, Wildlife & Parks (FWP), the state agency responsible for wildlife management, conducted three statewide surveys in 2012, 2017, and 2023 to track public attitudes toward wolves and wolf management. I led the full analysis for this project—building an ETL pipeline (Python + SQL + R) to harmonize data across waves, resolve survey drift, and deduplicate respondents; applying stratified sampling with post-stratification weights across 4 stakeholder groups (general public, landowners, deer hunters, wolf hunters); and developing survey-weighted logistic regression models to identify key predictors of tolerance.

    The analysis reduced a candidate set of 50+ variables to 5 deployable predictors through iterative feature selection, producing a parsimonious model that maintains predictive power while enabling rapid stakeholder profiling. Results are presented through interactive Tableau dashboards tracking longitudinal trends and a predictive modeling application, providing agency leadership and policymakers with clear, evidence-based insights to balance diverse interests across Montana's wolf-affected communities.

    Executive Summary

    Montanans' views on wolves have shifted markedly across three statewide surveys (2012, 2017, 2023). To track these trends, we built an ETL pipeline harmonizing 3 survey waves with deduplication logic, then applied post-stratification weights across 4 stakeholder groups (general public, landowners, deer hunters, wolf hunters) to ensure results represent Montana's adult population. Tolerance for wolves on the landscape has steadily increased among residents and deer hunters (+0.9 and +1.3 on a 1–5 scale). Landowners remain less tolerant overall, but their attitudes have also trended upward, suggesting a gradual broadening of acceptance across most groups.

    The 2023 wave expanded the lens—capturing first-hand wolf encounters, trust in agency management, and detailed policy preferences. Using survey-weighted logistic regression, we reduced a candidate set of 50+ variables to 5 key predictors that explain tolerance patterns across groups. Results show that distant sightings reliably build tolerance, while sightings near homes and reports of property damage drive the largest negative shifts. Wolf hunters stand out: their tolerance does not improve from positive encounters, underscoring the need for approaches beyond exposure-based strategies.

    Taken together, the findings highlight both opportunity and constraint: tolerance for wolf presence is broadening in the population at large, yet the most common encounters are not the ones that improve it. Results are presented through interactive Tableau dashboards tracking 10+ years of longitudinal trends and a predictive modeling application enabling rapid stakeholder profiling. These insights support targeted communication, rapid conflict response, and program design that balance biological realities with social dynamics— offering leadership clear, defensible guidance for navigating one of Montana's most contentious policy arenas.

    Stratified Oversample → Weighted Population

    We oversampled key groups for analysis, then applied survey weights so results represent Montana’s adult population. Oversampling gave us enough responses from smaller but policy-relevant groups (like landowners, deer hunters, and wolf hunters) to analyze their perspectives in detail. Weighting then rebalanced the dataset back to the actual demographic makeup of Montana, ensuring statewide averages weren’t distorted by the larger sample sizes from those groups. This combination provides both granular insight into stakeholder segments and defensible statewide estimates.

    Data Harmonization & ETL Pipeline

    The three survey waves used different instruments, variable naming conventions, and file formats, making direct comparison challenging. To create a unified analytical dataset, we built a multi-language ETL pipeline (Python + SQL + R) that:

    • Python (Pandas): Read raw SPSS files from each wave, standardized variable names, mapped response codes to common scales, and created shared household keys for tracking respondents across years [View notebook →]
    • SQL (PostgreSQL): Implemented deduplication logic to identify and resolve cases where the same individual appeared in multiple waves or strata. Applied rule-based prioritization (e.g., keep most recent response, flag known duplicates) and enforced referential integrity [View SQL script →]
    • R (tidyverse + survey package): Applied post-stratification weights, validated weighted distributions against known population benchmarks, and generated weighted summary statistics and confidence intervals for all reporting [View R script →]

    This pipeline transformed three disparate datasets into a single, analysis-ready table with consistent variable definitions, resolved duplicates, and properly weighted observations. All transformation logic is version-controlled and documented, ensuring reproducibility and transparency.

    🔧 Technical Details: Deduplication Logic

    Challenge: Some respondents appeared in multiple strata (e.g., a landowner who is also a deer hunter) or responded to multiple waves.

    Solution: SQL queries identified duplicates using household ID + demographics. When conflicts arose, we kept the most specific stratum (wolf hunter > deer hunter > landowner > general) and the most recent wave. Flagged cases were reviewed manually to ensure logical consistency.

    Statistical Modeling Approach

    To identify the strongest predictors of wolf tolerance, we used survey-weighted logistic regression with post-stratification weights applied throughout. This ensured that:

    • Parameter estimates reflect Montana's adult population, not just oversampled groups
    • Standard errors account for stratified sampling design
    • Predictions generalize to the broader public, not just survey respondents

    Feature Selection Process

    The 2023 survey included 50+ candidate predictors spanning demographics, wolf encounters, trust in agencies, hunting participation, and policy preferences. To build a deployable model, we applied stepwise feature selection combined with domain expertise:

    1. Univariate screening: Identified variables with significant bivariate associations with tolerance (p < 0.10)
    2. Multicollinearity check: Removed highly correlated predictors (VIF > 5) to avoid redundancy and unstable estimates
    3. Stepwise selection (AIC): Used backward elimination to retain only variables that improved model fit and maintained interpretability [View modeling notebook →]
    4. Domain validation: Reviewed final variable set with FWP biologists and social scientists to ensure practical relevance
    5. Cross-validation: Confirmed that the reduced model (5 predictors) maintained predictive accuracy (AUC > 0.78) while dramatically simplifying deployment

    The 5 final predictors were: (1) prior wolf encounters (type and proximity), (2) trust in FWP management, (3) hunting participation, (4) rural vs. urban residence, and (5) age. This parsimonious model enables rapid stakeholder profiling without requiring full survey administration— critical for real-time policy guidance.

    🔄 Why feature selection matters: A model with 50+ predictors is impractical for deployment (requires too much data collection) and prone to overfitting. Reducing to 5 key variables maintains predictive power while making the model actionable—stakeholders can assess tolerance using just 5 survey questions instead of 50+.

    Encounters: How They Shape Tolerance

    The chart below comes from a multivariate regression, meaning that each coefficient reflects the independent effect of that encounter type while controlling for the influence of the others. In other words, we can say how much tolerance shifts after seeing tracks, net of whether someone has also seen a wolf near their home, heard of property damage, etc.

    • Seeing tracks: a small but significant negative effect.
    • Seeing a wolf from afar: the clearest positive effect—distant sightings are reliably positive.
    • Seeing a wolf close to home: a strong negative effect.
    • Hearing about property damage: the largest negative effect overall.

    Plain Takeaways

    • Not all encounters are equal: distant sightings reliably build tolerance, while property damage erodes it the most.
    • The “home” context matters: sightings near where people live tilt strongly negative, regardless of tracks or damage.
    • Group contrasts are muted: landowners and the general public show similar patterns. Wolf hunters, however, don’t respond positively to any encounters — meaning tolerance in that group likely requires different levers than “making encounters feel safer.”

    Encounters: How Common Are They?

    Knowing which encounters matter most raises the next question: how often do people actually have them? The prevalence chart shows that while positive encounters (like seeing wolves from afar) do occur, the more common experiences tend to be neutral or negative—especially tracks and damage reports.

    This imbalance helps explain why tolerance grows slowly: many Montanans’ first-hand experiences skew toward reminders of risk rather than awe. And for wolf hunters, even when encounters occur, they rarely translate into positive shifts.

    Prevalence of encounters by group (tracks, howls, sightings, damage).

    Together, these visuals highlight a challenge for coexistence: the encounters most common on the landscape are not the ones that improve tolerance. Building acceptance may therefore depend less on exposure alone and more on how those experiences are interpreted, discussed, and managed.

    Recommendations

    Stakeholder Engagement & Messaging

    • Lead with distant-sighting storytelling: Distant wolf sightings consistently build tolerance. Agencies can emphasize these positive encounters in outreach and media.
    • Differentiate for wolf hunters: Since hunters don’t respond positively to encounters, messaging should focus on management clarity, herd health, and fairness in regulation.
    • Address near-home encounters: Sightings close to residences are strongly negative. Localized communication and rapid response can reduce fear and improve community relations.

    Conflict Mitigation & Response

    • Prioritize property-damage incidents: Damage reports drive the largest negative shifts. Timely investigation and transparent communication are key to limiting spillover distrust.
    • Proactive prevention near homes: Small steps—like deterrents or buffer practices—can prevent incidents that otherwise erode trust quickly.
    • Consistent communication playbook: Clear, fast messaging after conflicts can keep small events from fueling wider negativity.

    Program & Policy Opportunities

    • Create safe-distance viewing opportunities: Guided programs for observing wolves from afar can turn neutral curiosity into positive tolerance.
    • Pair deterrents with compensation: Offering tools alongside financial relief helps landowners feel supported while also reducing future conflicts.
    • Segmented hunter briefs: Concise, targeted updates for hunters can improve perceptions of fairness, even if tolerance levels don’t shift.

    Data & Tracking

    • Integrate encounter data: Linking survey data with field and hotline reports can help identify hotspots and patterns in real time.
    • Track sentiment over time: Shorter pulse surveys can detect changes in tolerance more quickly than 5-year waves.
    • Test communication frames: Comparing “awe” vs. “risk management” narratives helps refine messaging that resonates across different groups.

    Clarifying Questions, Assumptions, and Caveats

    Questions for Stakeholders Prior to Project Advancement

    • Experience Measurement Consistency: Should all wolf-related encounters (seeing tracks, hearing of damage, visual sightings) be standardized into a single index, or treated separately? How should we define the temporal window of an encounter (e.g., within the past year vs. lifetime experience)?
    • Contextual Variables: To what extent do demographic or geographic controls (e.g., rural vs. urban residence) need to be included alongside wolf encounters? Should “close to home” sightings be defined by distance bands (e.g., within 5 miles), or by self-reported perceptions?
    • Interpretation of Effects: How should small but statistically significant coefficients (e.g., seeing tracks) be communicated — as actionable insights or background context? Should distant sightings be emphasized as a reliable positive driver, even if the effect size is moderate?

    Assumptions

    • Linearity in Relationships: Logistic regressions assume linear relationships on the log-odds scale. Threshold effects of repeated damage are not captured.
    • Independence of Encounters: Each reported encounter is treated as independent. Overlap (e.g., tracks and damage at the same site) may bias estimates.
    • Population Representativeness: The sample is assumed to represent residents in wolf regions. Without survey weighting, generalizability may be limited.

    Caveats

    • Omitted Variables: Attitudes may also be shaped by cultural, political, or historical factors not included in the dataset.
    • Communication of Risk: Stakeholders may misinterpret coefficients as causal. Results should be framed as associations.
    • Survey Design Constraints: Recall bias may affect reported encounters, particularly rare or emotionally charged events like property damage.
