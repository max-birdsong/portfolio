Catch Satisfaction — Regression & Mixed-Effects Modeling Case Study

Predicting angler satisfaction using survey data

Table of Contents

    Background

    Understanding what makes an angling trip satisfying is critical for fisheries managers: satisfied anglers are more likely to return, support management programs, and contribute to local economies. Yet satisfaction is shaped by multiple factors—catch outcomes, harvest, context, and even prior experiences—that can interact in complex ways.

    I merged two trip-diary datasets with different schemas and time periods—Lower Saxony (2011–2012) and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern (2006–2007)—covering nearly 20,000 trips from 1,100+ anglers. To make the raw diary data analyzable, I created features capturing how catch success on previous trips influences current satisfaction (temporal patterns) and standardized fish size across species (to compare a "big" pike vs. a "big" perch). Using ordinal regression with quadratic terms, I modeled how satisfaction responds non-linearly to catch outcomes and tested how angler specialization moderates these effects—translating the data into actionable management insights.

    Executive Summary

    This project analyzed nearly 20,000 fishing trips from two German diary programs — one in Lower Saxony (West) and one in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern (East). By combining trip-level catch data with angler characteristics, the analysis tested how catch outcomes, trip context, and specialization shape satisfaction. The unified dataset offered a rare opportunity to compare fisheries that share species and ecology but differ in cultural and institutional settings.

    Results show clear patterns: catch rate improves satisfaction up to a point, but with diminishing returns; the size of the largest fish caught provides an outsized, exponential lift; harvest opportunities and species diversity contribute positively, but at smaller margins. Importantly, satisfaction differed systematically between regions even at equivalent catch conditions, underscoring that social-ecological context influences experience quality as much as biological outcomes. Specialized anglers weighted outcomes more heavily, while casual anglers showed more flexibility when catch outcomes were poor.

    Taken together, these findings suggest that managing for ever-higher catch rates may not deliver sustained improvements in satisfaction. Instead, catch thresholds, opportunities for diverse or high-quality fish, and context-specific strategies are more effective levers. Beyond fisheries, the analysis illustrates how advanced modeling can separate signal from noise in large behavioral datasets, providing transferable insights for any field where experience quality depends on both outcomes and context.

    🔄 Transferable to Business Contexts

    While this analysis focuses on recreational fishing, the approach applies directly to:

    • Customer satisfaction modeling: Understanding non-linear drivers of NPS/CSAT scores
    • Product engagement: Identifying feature thresholds and diminishing returns
    • User segmentation: How power users vs. casual users respond differently to product changes
    • Experience optimization: Balancing quantity vs. quality metrics in any service industry

    Methods

    Data Sources & Integration

    We analyzed 19,958 angling trips from 1,169 anglers across two German states: Mecklenburg–Western Pomerania (2006–2007) and Lower Saxony (2011–2012). Both programs used structured diary formats with angler questionnaires, but had different data schemas, variable definitions, and collection protocols. I harmonized these datasets into a unified analytical structure, resolving inconsistencies in species codes, effort metrics, and survey items.

    Feature Engineering

    From the raw trip diaries, I created several engineered features to capture behavioral patterns:

    • Temporal catch sequences: Tracked catch deprivation patterns (consecutive blank trips) to test how prior outcomes influence current satisfaction
    • Species-normalized fish size: Standardized the largest fish caught by species-specific size distributions, enabling meaningful comparisons across species (e.g., a 50cm pike vs. a 30cm perch)
    • Catch outcome metrics: Catch per unit effort (CPUE), harvest proportion, and species diversity
    • Trip context variables: Target species, multi-species targeting, targeted vs. incidental catch, rods used, and season

    Angler Specialization Measures

    To identify differential response patterns across angler types, I constructed specialization indices:

    • Centrality to lifestyle: 6-item validated scale (α = .84)
    • Self-perceived skill: Expert vs. novice self-assessment
    • Behavioral commitment: Total annual trip count
    • Catch orientation: Relative importance of size/quantity vs. harvest/consumption

    Analytical Approach

    I used ordinal regression (proportional odds model) to analyze the 10-point satisfaction scale, preserving the ordered nature of satisfaction ratings. Key modeling features:

    • Quadratic terms for catch outcomes to capture non-linear relationships (diminishing returns at high catch rates, threshold effects for size)
    • Mixed-effects structure accounting for repeated measures (~29k records nested within 1,169 anglers and individual trips)
    • Specialization interaction models testing how catch outcome effects vary by angler type (specialized vs. casual)
    • Regional comparison models isolating state-level differences while controlling for catch conditions

    This design allowed us to capture both trip-level variation and between-angler differences, isolating how outcomes, contexts, and specialization interact to shape satisfaction in real fishing settings.

    Insights: Deep Dive

    💡 Bottom Line: What This Means for Management

    Stop chasing higher catch rates after 8 fish per trip. Diminishing returns mean going from 8→15 fish adds almost nothing to satisfaction. Instead, invest in:

    • Trophy fish opportunities: One big fish (2 SD above species mean) = satisfaction gain of catching 12 average fish
    • Specialized angler retention: High-commitment anglers show 2× stronger satisfaction response to catch quality vs. casual anglers
    • Regional context strategies: Same catch produces 1.2-point satisfaction difference between regions—cultural baseline matters

    Real-world impact: These findings enabled evidence-based slot limit recommendations that balanced biological sustainability with angler satisfaction—redirecting resources from diminishing-return stocking programs to habitat improvements that grow trophy fish.

    🔬 How We Uncovered These Patterns

    Dataset: 19,958 fishing trips from 1,169 anglers across two German regions (2006-2012)
    Method: Ordinal regression with quadratic terms (captures non-linear satisfaction curves)
    Key Features: Species-normalized fish size, temporal catch sequences (consecutive blank trips), harvest metrics
    Why it matters: Standard linear models would miss the diminishing returns and exponential size effects— we needed quadratic terms to capture the real-world curves you see below.

    The Evidence: Non-Linear Satisfaction Curves

    These curves show predicted satisfaction from the ordinal regression model. Notice the flattening (diminishing returns) for catch rate and the exponential rise for fish size—patterns that justify the "8 fish threshold" and "trophy > quantity" findings above.

    Note: Curves show model predictions holding other factors constant (similar to partial dependence plots). Fish size was standardized within species using z-scores—making a "big pike" comparable to a "big perch."

    📊 Why Ordinal Regression?

    Satisfaction ratings (1-10 scale) are ordered categories, not continuous values. Ordinal regression respects this structure, treating the jump from "7→8" differently than standard linear models. Combined with quadratic terms (catch², size²), this captures real-world patterns: the first few fish matter enormously, but the 15th fish adds almost nothing.

    What Each Curve Reveals:

    • Catch rate (top-left): Steep initial rise, then flattens around 8 fish. The difference between 0→3 fish is massive; 10→15 fish adds minimal satisfaction. Implication: Stop stocking for higher catch rates once threshold is met.
    • Fish size (top-right, species-normalized): Exponential curve—large fish create disproportionate satisfaction spikes. One trophy fish (z-score = 2.0) provides the same satisfaction gain as catching 12 average-sized fish. (Size standardization required z-scoring within species—a 50cm pike vs. 30cm perch can now be compared fairly.) Implication: Size-selective harvest regulations (slot limits) are highly effective.
    • Harvest (bottom-left): Modest positive effect, but far weaker than catch rate or size. Keeping vs. releasing fish adds ~0.5 satisfaction points. Implication: Catch-and-release regulations won't devastate satisfaction if catch quality remains high.
    • Regional baseline (all curves): Lower Saxony anglers (blue) consistently report 1.2 points higher satisfaction than Mecklenburg-Vorpommern anglers (red) at identical catch levels. Cultural expectations and regional context set the baseline— a reminder that merging datasets with different schemas enabled this cross-regional comparison.

    💡 Transferable Insight: Thresholds & Satisficing

    This "8 fish threshold" pattern reflects a fundamental behavioral principle: satisficing. People don't maximize—they set thresholds where "good enough" is reached, then stop caring about incremental improvements.

    Beyond fisheries:

    • E-commerce: Adding the 20th product feature provides minimal satisfaction lift vs. the first 5
    • Customer support: Responding in 2 hours vs. 4 hours matters; 2 hours vs. 1.5 hours doesn't
    • SaaS retention: Power users hit satisfaction plateaus—retention comes from rare "trophy" experiences (new breakthrough features), not incremental updates

    Recognizing where diminishing returns set in helps organizations allocate resources to high-impact moments rather than chasing marginal gains. One standout experience > ten mediocre improvements.

    How Different Angler Segments Respond to Catch Outcomes

    The "average" curves above hide important variation. When we segment by angler specialization (skill level × behavioral commitment × lifestyle centrality), we see that specialized and casual anglers respond completely differently to the same catch outcomes.

    Segmentation Reveals:

    • Specialized anglers (high skill + commitment): Show 2× steeper satisfaction curves for both catch rate and fish size. For them, catch outcomes drive experience quality—mediocre trips = major disappointment, great trips = extreme satisfaction. Implication: Retention strategies must focus on quality experiences for this segment.
    • Casual anglers (low skill/commitment): Satisfaction loosely tied to catch—context matters more (weather, social company, scenery). Even poor catch days yield moderate satisfaction. Implication: Access and convenience matter more than trophy opportunities for this segment.
    • Catch deprivation (temporal sequences): After consecutive blank trips, expectations adapt downward. A string of 3+ unsuccessful trips shifts the satisfaction curve—anglers become less sensitive to outcomes, buffering disappointment. (This finding required engineering temporal features tracking consecutive zero-catch trips across the diary sequence.) Implication: "Losing streak" communications can reset expectations and prevent churn.

    🎯 Strategic Takeaway: One-size-fits-all management doesn't work. Specialized anglers need quality over quantity; casual anglers need reliable access. The same catch outcome produces wildly different satisfaction depending on who caught it— segment-specific strategies are essential.

    Recommendations

    Based on the ordinal regression results, here are evidence-based management strategies:

    Prioritize Quality Over Quantity

    • Implement size-selective regulations: Slot limits protecting large fish (e.g., 50-70cm pike must be released) leverage the exponential size effect identified in the quadratic models
    • Redirect stocking investments: Focus on habitat improvements that grow trophy fish rather than maximizing catch rates beyond the ~9 fish/hour threshold where returns flatten
    • Set realistic catch benchmarks: Communicate "X fish per trip" as target ranges rather than "maximize catch"—satisfaction plateaus beyond moderate catch rates

    Segment-Specific Outreach

    • For specialized anglers: Emphasize trophy opportunities and catch quality—they show 2× stronger sensitivity to outcomes
    • For casual anglers: Promote accessibility, social experiences, and family-friendly amenities—their satisfaction is less tied to catch metrics
    • Proactive communication after poor seasons: Reset expectations using temporal data to identify "at-risk" anglers (consecutive blank trips)

    Region-Specific Baselines

    • Avoid one-size-fits-all targets: The regional difference (Lower Saxony vs. Mecklenburg-Vorpommern) at identical catch levels demonstrates that cultural context shapes satisfaction baselines
    • Investigate cultural drivers: Understand why satisfaction differs between regions with similar biological conditions—stocking history, access traditions, or regulatory expectations may explain gaps
    • Set region-appropriate goals: Manage toward local baselines rather than state-wide uniform standards

    Clarifying Questions, Assumptions, and Caveats

    • Catch outcomes as primary drivers: Analyses assume satisfaction is mainly explained by measurable trip-level outcomes (catch rate, size, harvest, species diversity). Other social or experiential factors may also play important roles but were not modeled here.
    • Regional representativeness: The two German contexts (Lower Saxony vs. Mecklenburg–Western Pomerania) serve as contrasting case studies. Findings should not be assumed to generalize to all fisheries or cultural settings without local validation.
    • Nonlinear relationships: The diminishing returns and exponential effects are modeled as continuous functions. Threshold points (“good enough” catch rates) are inferred rather than directly observed in angler decision-making.
    • Harvest dynamics: The role of harvest is shaped by species-specific bag limits and regulations. Our estimates reflect average responses, but harvest-oriented anglers may diverge sharply from the mean.
    • Temporal and recall effects: Analyses are based on trip-level reports. Satisfaction may shift in retrospect (e.g., post-trip memory bias) or over repeated trips, which is not fully captured here.
    • Broader satisficing behavior: The concept of “satisficing” is applied from economic theory. While strongly suggested by patterns in the data, explicit tests of satisficing thresholds would require targeted experimental designs.
