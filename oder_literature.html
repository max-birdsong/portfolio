Literature Review

Theoretical context and empirical foundations for Oder River ecosystem services research

This review synthesizes key literature across five domains: (1) recreational fisheries social science synthesis (most recent), (2) the 2022 Oder River ecological disaster and its impacts, (3) cultural ecosystem services valuation methods, (4) relational values and human dimensions frameworks, and (5) harmful algal blooms and aquatic ecosystem disturbances. The review identifies gaps that the OderTogether research project addresses.

1. Recreational Fisheries Social Science: Current State of the Field

The Definitive Synthesis

The most comprehensive and current synthesis of recreational fisheries research provides the theoretical and methodological foundation for human dimensions work.

"Understanding Recreational Fishers: Disciplinary and Interdisciplinary Approaches for Fisheries Management" - This 848-page open-access volume represents the field's most authoritative synthesis, bringing together leading scholars across social psychology, economics, ecology, and management. The book presents cutting-edge frameworks for understanding angler behavior, measuring social-psychological constructs, optimizing surveys and creel studies, analyzing qualitative data, and facilitating stakeholder engagement. Key chapters directly relevant to OderTogether include: (1) Social Psychology chapter (Landon et al. 2026) presenting a novel integrated conceptual framework linking values, beliefs, attitudes, and behavior; (2) Measurement Instruments chapter (Kyle et al. 2026) providing validated scales and psychometric best practices; (3) Creel Survey Optimization (de Kerckhove et al. 2026) detailing sample size requirements and bias mitigation; (4) Content Analysis (Kochalski et al. 2026) providing methods for analyzing open-ended responses; (5) Knowledge Co-production (Arlinghaus et al. 2026) outlining the four-step framework for stakeholder workshops; and (6) Survey Guidelines (Vaske et al. 2026) establishing design standards. This volume cites Birdsong et al. (2021) as THE key reference on angler satisfaction, positioning the OderTogether PI's prior work as foundational to current understanding.
Pope, K.L., Arlinghaus, R., Hunt, L.M., Lynch, A.J., & van Poorten, B.T. (Eds.) (2026). Fish & Fisheries Series Vol. 45, Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-99739-6

Angler Satisfaction: State of Knowledge

Understanding what drives angler satisfaction is central to fisheries management, as satisfaction reflects whether management achieves social objectives.

"Recreational angler satisfaction: what drives it?" - Meta-analysis of 75 studies (N=55,341 anglers) examining satisfaction predictors. Key findings: (1) catch outcomes matter (catch rate, catch size, keep fish), but effects are moderated by angler characteristics and trip context; (2) specialized anglers show weaker catch-satisfaction relationships than casual anglers; (3) satisfaction is multidimensional with physical (catch), cognitive (learning, achievement), and affective (enjoyment, relaxation) components; (4) social and environmental factors (companionship, scenery, facilities) contribute substantially to overall satisfaction. This work is cited throughout Pope et al. (2026) as the definitive review and provides the foundation for understanding post-disaster satisfaction dynamics in the Oder context.
Birdsong, M., Hunt, L.M., & Arlinghaus, R. (2021). Fish and Fisheries, 22(4), 682-706. https://doi.org/10.1111/faf.12545 [Winner: Most Cited Paper of 2021]
Recent Danish study examining catch-satisfaction relationships using citizen science data (N=15,000+ trips). Found that satisfaction is highly context-dependent, with trip characteristics, motivations, and catch outcomes interacting in complex ways. Supports meta-analytic findings that simple catch-satisfaction relationships are insufficient—context matters.
Gundelund, C., Arlinghaus, R., Birdsong, M., et al. (2022). Journal of Environmental Management, 321, 115942.

Specialization and Psychological Commitment

Angler specialization captures heterogeneity in engagement, motivations, and behavioral patterns, yet measurement has been inconsistent across studies.

Pope et al. (2026) synthesis identifies specialization measurement as an "ongoing concern" with "large variation in how the concept, or sub-dimensions, have been measured" (Landon et al. 2026). New recommendation: If researchers do not measure all three dimensions (behavioral commitment, psychological commitment, skill/cognition), the term "specialization" should not be used. Instead, researchers should specify which dimension they measured (e.g., "centrality" or "psychological commitment"). This addresses decades of confusion in the literature where studies measuring single dimensions claimed to assess "specialization."
Landon, A.C., Schroeder, S.A., Steffen, S.F., & Arlinghaus, R. (2026). Social Psychology for Understanding Recreational Fishers and Fisheries. In Pope et al. (Eds.), pp. 157-196.
Centrality (psychological commitment) predicts conservation-oriented behaviors including voluntary catch-and-release. Highly centrally-involved anglers are "more sensitive to fishing-related stimuli" and "more likely to engage in protective behaviors" during periods of ecological stress.
Sutton, S.G., & Ditton, R.B. (2001). Centrality and catch-release behavior in bluefin tuna fishery.
Different specialization dimensions predict different outcomes: centrality predicts habitat/harvest regulation attitudes, while behavioral commitment predicts attitudes toward commercial fisheries. This supports treating dimensions separately rather than creating composite scores.
Slaton, R., et al. (2023). Specialization dimensions have differential effects.

2. The 2022 Oder River Disaster

Ecological and Social Context

The summer 2022 mass die-off in the Oder River killed hundreds of tons of fish, mussels, and other species, representing one of Europe's most severe recent freshwater ecological disasters. Multiple sources document the event's scope and ongoing uncertainties.

"The Oder river has been contaminated. Many of the fish are dead. The German government describes the death of tons of fish in the Oder river as an 'environmental disaster.' ... The German and Polish authorities do not yet know what caused it."
Porcelaincockroach, 2022 (Reddit discussion thread)
"Fishing in the wake of the Oder River disaster shows no sign of... [recovery]"
Title fragment, 2022

IGB Potsdam issued policy recommendations emphasizing restoration over engineering fixes, highlighting the need for cross-border coordination and stakeholder engagement.

"The future of the River Oder" - IGB Policy Brief addressing management priorities and restoration pathways following the disaster.
IGB Policy Brief, 2023

Lessons and Management Implications

Post-disaster analyses emphasize the importance of early warning systems, transboundary coordination, and understanding social dimensions of recovery.

"Lessons From the Oder Environmental Disaster" - examining failures in monitoring, communication, and cross-border cooperation that contributed to delayed response.
2023
EU analysis of the ecological disaster emphasizes need for improved governance structures and coordinated management between Germany and Poland.
European Commission, 2023

3. Cultural Ecosystem Services Valuation

Conceptual Frameworks

Cultural ES are notoriously difficult to quantify but increasingly recognized as central to human well-being. Multiple frameworks guide their assessment.

"Ecosystem services valuation: a review of concepts, systems, methods and practices" - comprehensive review of ES valuation approaches including stated preference, revealed preference, and non-monetary methods.
Zandebasiri et al., 2023
"A systematic review of cultural ecosystem services and human well-being" - identifies recreation, aesthetic appreciation, sense of place, and cultural heritage as key dimensions, noting persistent measurement challenges.
Kosanic and Petzold, 2020

Empirical Applications

Recent studies demonstrate diverse approaches to cultural ES assessment in aquatic contexts, with particularly relevant methodologies emerging from IGB research on threatened fish species.

"Perceived socio-cultural ecosystem services provided by wild Atlantic salmon populations in four European countries" - uses large-scale online survey (N=2,298) across France, Germany, Norway, and Sweden to elicit importance ratings for 13 ES items. Exploratory factor analysis groups items into three categories: intrinsic-ecological (existence value, bioindicator function, genetic diversity), extractive (income, tourism, fishing, heritage), and aesthetic-educational services. Key finding: ES perception is "co-produced by ecology and society"—countries with healthier salmon populations (Norway) show higher valuation across all categories. Demonstrates that improving ecological status can foster development of perceived ecosystem services. Methodological note: This approach establishes IGB's framework for multi-country ES surveys using factor analysis and Likert scales to capture diverse value dimensions.
Kochalski, Riepe & Arlinghaus, 2022 (Aquatic Ecosystem Health & Management)
"Factors affecting cultural ecosystem services use in Vilnius" - examines how personal characteristics, place attachment, and accessibility shape CES use and valuation.
Pinto et al., 2023
"Coastal cultural ecosystem services and adolescents' subjective well-being" - links specific CES (e.g., recreation, aesthetic appreciation) to mental health outcomes, demonstrating welfare relevance.
Cabana et al., 2024

4. Relational Values and Human Dimensions

Beyond Instrumental and Intrinsic Values

The IPBES framework introduces relational values as a third category alongside instrumental (use-based) and intrinsic (inherent worth) values, capturing relationships, responsibilities, and meanings.

"Stephen Kellert's development and contribution of relational values" - traces evolution of relational value concept, emphasizing emotional and spiritual connections to nature beyond utility or preservation.
Ross et al., 2018

This framework is particularly relevant for post-disaster contexts where sense of place, cultural identity, and trust in institutions become salient.

Cross-Border and Cross-Cultural Considerations

Transboundary resource management requires understanding how values differ across cultural and political boundaries.

Interreg Brandenburg-Poland programme documentation emphasizes need for joint projects that build mutual understanding and collaborative capacity between German and Polish stakeholders.
Programme / Interreg Brandenburg – Poland

5. Harmful Algal Blooms and Ecosystem Disturbances

Impacts on Cultural Ecosystem Services

Research on harmful algal blooms (HABs) and similar disturbances provides relevant analogies for understanding Oder disaster impacts.

"Harmful algal blooms: the impacts on cultural ecosystem services" - documents how HAB events disrupt recreation, aesthetic enjoyment, sense of place, and therapeutic interactions with coastal settings. Economic impacts include lost tourism, restaurant/hotel revenue, beach cleanups, and reduced property values.
Willis et al., 2018
"Impacts of Florida red tides on coastal communities" - examines social and economic consequences including disrupted livelihoods, psychological stress, and erosion of place attachment.
Backer, 2009
"A Time Series Approach to Estimating the Economic Impacts of..." - develops methods for tracking recovery trajectories and valuing lost services during and after disturbance events.
Oh and Ditton, 2008

6. Fisheries Closures and Socio-Cultural Impacts

Understanding Angler and Community Responses

"Economic and sociocultural impacts of fisheries closures" - examines how fishing restrictions affect not just harvest but also identity, social networks, and quality of life for fishing-dependent communities.
Ritzman et al., 2018

While the Oder wasn't formally closed, reduced catch rates and health concerns create similar dynamics of loss, uncertainty, and adaptation.

Research Gaps Addressed by OderTogether

Despite extensive literature on ecosystem services and aquatic disturbances, several gaps remain:

Gap 1: Cross-Border Cultural ES Comparison

Most ES studies are single-country. Few examine how values differ across national borders sharing the same resource, particularly in post-disturbance contexts. OderTogether's German-Polish comparison addresses this directly.

Gap 2: Tradeoff Methods in Cultural ES

Importance ratings often show ceiling effects (everything is important). Few studies use forced-choice or budget allocation methods to reveal relative priorities. OderTogether combines importance ratings with explicit tradeoff measures.

Gap 3: Trust and Governance Legitimacy

ES literature rarely integrates trust in institutions or stakeholder legitimacy, yet these are critical for implementation. OderTogether explicitly measures trust and connects it to ES priorities.

Gap 4: Integration of Stated and Revealed Preferences

Most studies use either surveys (stated preferences) or behavioral data (revealed preferences), not both. Linking resident ES survey to catch log study allows validation of stated priorities against actual fishing behavior.

Gap 5: Recovery Trajectory Documentation

Limited longitudinal data on how perceptions and values change during ecosystem recovery from major disturbances. OderTogether baseline enables future tracking of recovery in both ecological and social dimensions.

Gap 6: Methodological Rigor in Cultural ES Assessment

Many ES studies use ad hoc survey items rather than validated, psychometrically-tested scales. Pope et al. (2026) emphasizes use of established measurement instruments with documented reliability and validity. OderTogether applies this standard through use of validated scales (Kyle et al. 2026 compendium), proper translation protocols (TRAPD method), and rigorous pilot testing, setting a methodological benchmark for transboundary ES research.

Synthesis: Theoretical Positioning

The OderTogether project sits at the intersection of multiple literatures and applies cutting-edge methods from the most recent synthesis of recreational fisheries social science (Pope et al. 2026):

  • Recreational fisheries social science - Applying the integrated social-psychological framework (Landon et al. 2026) to understand angler responses to ecological stress, using validated measurement instruments (Kyle et al. 2026) and optimized creel survey protocols (de Kerckhove et al. 2026)
  • Ecosystem services valuation - Building on IGB's established ES survey methodology (Kochalski et al. 2022 salmon study) while adding rigorous content analysis of open-ended responses (Kochalski et al. 2026)
  • Relational values - Expanding beyond instrumental use to capture place attachment, identity, and cultural meaning in post-disaster context
  • Environmental disturbance impacts - Drawing on HAB and fisheries closure literature to understand social consequences of the 2022 disaster, with methodological grounding in satisfaction research (Birdsong et al. 2021, featured as key reference in Pope et al. 2026)
  • Cross-border governance - Informing transboundary management through stakeholder co-production (Arlinghaus et al. 2026 four-step framework) combined with empirical understanding of value differences and trust dynamics
  • Methodological innovation - Integrating stated preferences (ES survey, creel survey) with revealed preferences (catch logs) following best practices for survey design (Vaske et al. 2026) and translation (TRAPD protocol)

By combining these threads and applying the most current methodological standards from Pope et al. (2026), the research generates actionable knowledge for sustainable, socially-legitimate Oder River management while contributing methodological advances in cultural ES assessment, cross-border natural resource governance, and post-disaster recovery assessment. The work builds directly on the PI's foundational satisfaction research (Birdsong et al. 2021), which is cited throughout the field's definitive synthesis as THE reference for understanding what drives angler well-being.
