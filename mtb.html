Mountain Biker Preferences — Exploratory Factor Analysis Project

Dimensionality reduction: 82 survey items reduced to 13 interpretable factors

Table of Contents

    Background

    In 2023, our team surveyed nearly 600 riders across Alabama to assess mountain biking participation, motivations, and setting preferences. I co-led the analysis—designing the survey instrument with 82 attitudinal items, cleaning responses, and applying exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to reduce this high-dimensional data into 13 interpretable factors capturing rider "push" motivations (5 factors from 25 items) and destination "pull" attributes (8 factors from 57 items).

    This dimensionality reduction revealed the underlying structure in rider preferences, transforming 82 individual survey items into meaningful, interpretable dimensions. The resulting factors show distinct rider segments and how internal motivations connect to preferred destination features—providing trail developers and managers with clear, data-driven guidance on how to align facilities, trails, and community infrastructure with diverse rider needs.

    Executive Summary

    Mountain biking has become one of the fastest-growing forms of outdoor recreation, and our 2023 statewide survey of nearly 600 Alabama riders captured detailed insights into participation, motivations, and setting preferences through 82 survey items. Riders reported a median of 50 trail days per year—well above national averages—underscoring the sport's strong and engaged user base.

    Using exploratory factor analysis, we reduced the 82 items into 13 interpretable factors (5 "push" motivations + 8 "pull" destination attributes), explaining 69% and 62% of variance respectively. This dimensionality reduction revealed distinct rider segments: Health-focused riders prioritize proximity, nature, and relaxation. Thrill-seekers and competitors demand advanced features and multiple trails close to home. Socially motivated riders value novelty and group experiences, traveling further and rating camping facilities highly. These differences highlight how motivation shapes destination choice.

    Taken together, the findings show that aligning facilities and infrastructure with diverse rider motives is key to sustaining growth. For communities and land managers, the factor structure provides clear, evidence-based guidance on when to emphasize natural settings, when to build advanced technical features, and how to attract bike tourism through amenities and community infrastructure.

    Methods

    Survey Design & Data Collection

    We designed and distributed an online survey in early 2023, promoted through SORBA chapters, Alabama State Parks, and social media. The instrument included 82 attitudinal items across five domains: rider motivations (25 items), satisfaction with current conditions, setting preferences (57 items), destinations visited, and demographics. The survey yielded 593 responses from a broad spectrum of riders, with attitudinal items rated on anchored 5- and 7-point Likert scales.

    Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

    To reduce dimensionality and reveal underlying structure, we applied exploratory factor analysis separately to the motivation items ("push" factors) and destination attribute items ("pull" factors). This approach transformed the 82 individual survey items into 13 interpretable latent factors.

    Key EFA specifications:

    • Extraction method: Principal axis factoring (PAF) to account for common variance
    • Rotation: Varimax (orthogonal) rotation to maximize factor interpretability
    • Factor selection: Combination of Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1), scree plot inflection points, and interpretability of factor structures
    • Push factors (motivations): 25 items reduced to 5 factors explaining ~69% of variance
    • Pull factors (destination attributes): 57 items reduced to 8 factors explaining ~62% of variance
    • Factor reliability: Cronbach's alpha for each factor ranged from 0.72 to 0.89, indicating good to excellent internal consistency
    • Factor scores: Computed via regression method for use in subsequent analyses

    Subsequent Analyses

    Using the derived factor scores, we then applied regression models to examine how rider motives (push factors) relate to preferred destination settings (pull factors), and correlation analysis to identify the strongest drivers of overall satisfaction. Finally, we used a revealed preference model, linking actual visitation patterns across 28 major Alabama trail systems to distance, trail features, facilities, and community characteristics. This provided both stated preferences and observed behavior to inform management decisions.

    Insights: Deep Dive

    💡 Bottom Line: What Drives Mountain Biking Preferences

    Exploratory factor analysis reduced 82 survey items into 13 interpretable factors, revealing the underlying structure of rider motivations and destination preferences:

    • 5 "push" factors from 25 motivation items (69% variance explained): Health & Nature, Mastery, Novelty, Social Interaction, Competition
    • 8 "pull" factors from 57 destination items (62% variance explained): Trail Features, Park Facilities, Community Infrastructure, Natural Setting, Advanced Features, Amenities, Camping, Entertainment
    • Distinct rider segments: Health-focused riders prioritize nature and proximity; mastery-driven riders demand technical features and community; social riders value amenities and novelty

    Why this matters: Instead of designing for an "average rider," managers can now target specific segments—building advanced features for mastery-driven riders, emphasizing scenery for health-focused riders, and creating social infrastructure for community-oriented riders.

    Push Factors: What Motivates Riders (5 Factors from 25 Items)

    Factor analysis of 25 motivation items revealed 5 underlying dimensions explaining ~69% of variance. These "push factors" capture why people ride—health, mastery, exploration, social connection, or competition. Each factor represents a distinct value proposition that mountain biking offers to different rider segments.

    Push factors: Health & Nature dominated overall, with novelty and mastery also standing out as core motivations.

    The 5 Motivation Factors:

    • Factor 1: Health & Nature (highest mean score): Items loading on this factor included physical fitness, stress relief, time outdoors, and connection with nature. Riders scoring high on this factor view mountain biking as a wellness and restorative activity. This was the most common motivation across the sample.
    • Factor 2: Competency–Mastery: This factor captured items related to skill development, challenge, pushing limits, and technical progression. Mastery-driven riders seek opportunities to improve and test themselves—they're motivated by growth, not just exercise.
    • Factor 3: Novelty: Items loading here emphasized exploration, variety, discovering new trails, and experiencing diverse landscapes. Novelty-seeking riders are motivated by discovery and change—they're likely to travel further and try new destinations.
    • Factor 4: Social Interaction: This factor included items about group riding, belonging to a community, shared experiences, and social connection. Socially-motivated riders value the relational aspects of the sport—riding is about people as much as trails.
    • Factor 5: Competition (lowest mean score): Items focused on racing, performance comparison, and competitive achievement. While this was the least common motivation, it represents a dedicated niche segment for whom events and ranking matter.
    🔄 Transferable insight: Customer segmentation based on underlying motivations (not just demographics) reveals why people engage with a product/service. Health-motivated riders and mastery-motivated riders have completely different needs—just as customers buying gym memberships for "weight loss" vs. "powerlifting" need different programs. EFA uncovers these latent segments from survey data.

    Pull Factors: What Destinations Offer (8 Factors from 57 Items)

    Factor analysis of 57 destination attribute items revealed 8 underlying dimensions explaining ~62% of variance. These "pull factors" capture what destinations provide—from core trail quality to supporting infrastructure and amenities. Each factor represents a dimension along which destinations can differentiate themselves.

    Pull factors: Trail features and park facilities emerged as the strongest overall drivers of destination appeal.

    The 8 Destination Factors:

    • Factor 1: Trail Features (highest mean score): Mileage, variety, well-designed singletrack, and low crowding. This is the core product—trail quality is the foundation of any destination. Crowding negatively impacts this factor, highlighting that capacity management matters as much as miles built.
    • Factor 2: Park Facilities: Signage, maps, parking, restrooms, and consistent maintenance. These are hygiene factors—their absence is noticed and detracts from experience, but their presence enables smooth visits rather than creating "wow" moments.
    • Factor 3: Community Infrastructure: Local bike shops, active clubs, welcoming culture, and organized events. Social ecosystem matters—destinations with strong community support encourage repeat visitation and word-of-mouth promotion.
    • Factor 4: Natural Setting: Scenery, wildlife, pristine feel, and connection with nature. This factor elevates rides from recreation to restoration—especially important for health-motivated riders.
    • Factor 5: Advanced Features: Downhill runs, flow trails, jumps, technical terrain. These attract progression-oriented riders seeking challenge and skill development— critical for mastery-motivated segments.
    • Factor 6: Park Amenities: Showers, toilets, picnic areas, water access. These provide comfort and convenience, extending visit length and making destinations family-friendly.
    • Factor 7: Camping: Developed campsites, primitive options, RV access. This enables multi-day stays and tourism—especially attractive to novelty-seeking riders traveling from out of area.
    • Factor 8: Entertainment & Social Offerings: Proximity to food, breweries, nightlife, and town character. These lifestyle amenities make destinations appealing for groups, events, and bike tourism—transforming a trail visit into a weekend destination.

    🎯 Why 8 factors matter: Destinations can't excel on all dimensions simultaneously— budget and land constraints force choices. The 8-factor structure shows which attributes cluster together (e.g., camping + entertainment vs. advanced features + community). Managers can now strategically invest in 2-3 factor dimensions that align with their target rider segments, rather than trying to be "everything to everyone."

    How Motivations Connect to Destination Preferences

    Using the derived factor scores, we ran regression models linking rider motivations (push) to destination attribute preferences (pull). The heatmap below shows standardized β coefficients— darker cells indicate stronger connections between why people ride and what destinations they prefer.

    Heatmap of standardized β coefficients: darker cells represent stronger, more consistent push–pull connections.

    Key Push–Pull Relationships:

    • Health & Nature → Trail Features + Natural Setting: Wellness-oriented riders strongly prefer scenic landscapes, well-maintained trails, and environmental quality. Trail upkeep and nature immersion are critical for this largest segment. (β coefficients: 0.42 for Trail Features, 0.51 for Natural Setting)
    • Mastery → Advanced Features + Community Infrastructure: Skill-driven riders showed the strongest overall effects, with clear preference for technical terrain, downhill sections, and strong local bike shops/clubs/events. Advanced trail design + community support create a progression-friendly ecosystem. (β coefficients: 0.58 for Advanced Features, 0.44 for Community)
    • Novelty → Trail Variety + Camping: Exploration-oriented riders valued diverse mileage, unique features, and overnight stay options. Destinations offering variety and multi-day experiences attract this segment. (β: 0.39 for Trail Features, 0.47 for Camping)
    • Social Interaction → Amenities + Community: Riders motivated by connection prioritized gathering spaces, welcoming culture, and facilities supporting group rides. Even modest investments in social infrastructure (picnic areas, event spaces) deliver strong returns for this segment. (β: 0.41 for Amenities, 0.36 for Community)
    • Competition → Weak/Mixed Effects: Competitive motivations showed limited influence on destination preferences—this segment is niche and their preferences overlap with mastery-driven riders. Focus resources elsewhere unless specifically targeting racing events.
    🔄 Transferable insight: Customer preferences aren't random—they're structured by underlying motivations. Just as health-motivated riders prefer natural settings while mastery-motivated riders prefer technical features, e-commerce customers motivated by "convenience" prefer different product attributes than those motivated by "status." Factor analysis + regression reveals these preference structures, enabling targeted product development and marketing.

    Recommendations

    Based on the 13-factor structure revealed through exploratory factor analysis, the findings point to clear, actionable priorities for trail managers, park staff, and community partners. The most effective strategies will match local investments with the motives of target riders, while ensuring baseline quality across all destinations.

    • Prioritize Trail Quality & Scenery (Factors 1 & 4): Well-maintained mileage, natural landscapes, and signage are foundational. These appeal broadly, especially to riders motivated by health, nature, and stress relief.
    • Build for Mastery (Factor 5 + Factor 3): Include advanced features, downhill sections, and technical challenges to retain skilled riders. Pair this with strong local infrastructure (shops, clubs, events) to foster progression and community.
    • Offer Variety & Overnight Options (Factors 1 & 7): Develop trail systems with diverse mileage, loops, and nearby camping to satisfy novelty-oriented riders who seek exploration and multi-day experiences.
    • Strengthen Social & Community Infrastructure (Factors 3 & 6): Invest in amenities like gathering spaces, group ride facilities, and welcoming culture initiatives. These elements resonate with socially-motivated riders and help build repeat visitation.
    • Calibrate for Competition: Competitive motives were weaker overall. Specialized race events or segments can still engage this niche, but they should not overshadow broader investments in trail quality and community.

    Taken together, these steps support a portfolio approach to trail planning: ensuring every destination meets core quality standards, while selectively emphasizing the features that align with the dominant motives of local and visiting riders. This strategy maximizes both participation and satisfaction, and positions communities to capture the growing demand for mountain biking in Alabama and beyond.

    Clarifying Questions, Assumptions, Caveats

    Like any data-driven analysis, these insights rest on certain assumptions and have boundaries that managers should keep in mind. They don’t undermine the results, but they do frame how best to use them.

    • Survey coverage: Results reflect responses from Alabama riders in 2023. Patterns may shift in other regions or with broader samples.
    • Motives are latent: Push factors were distilled from survey items via factor analysis. They capture underlying themes, but labels like “Health & Nature” are interpretive.
    • Self-reported behavior: Stated preferences describe what riders say they value, not always what they do. That said, the patterns aligned well with revealed preferences.
    • Snapshot in time: Data represent a single survey wave. Rider motives and visitation patterns could evolve with new infrastructure, social trends, or demographics.
    • Statistical thresholds: Reported relationships are significant at conventional levels (BH-adjusted p < .05), but smaller or more localized effects may not appear here.

    For managers, the takeaway is clear: use these findings as reliable directional guidance rather than fixed rules. They highlight where investment is most likely to pay off, while leaving space for local context, future monitoring, and rider feedback.
