Influence of Social Identity — Data Analysis Project

Survey data, latent variable modeling, and reproducible analysis

Table of Contents

    Background

    Grizzly bear conservation in Montana is shaped not only by biological realities, but also by deep social divisions. Hunters and nonhunters often hold sharply different views about grizzlies, and these differences go beyond demographics or direct encounters. They are rooted in social identity—how people see themselves and their in-groups in relation to wildlife and policy.

    To investigate these dynamics, we used a statewide survey of 1,758 Montana residents to examine how experiences with grizzlies (from distant sightings to reports of property damage) are filtered through identity-based lenses. Applying structural equation modeling (SEM), we tested whether hunter identity changes how people interpret experiences, form attitudes, and ultimately decide whether the grizzly population is "too large" or "too small." The results provide defensible insight into why support and opposition to carnivore management remain polarized—and how Montana's social landscape will shape the success of conservation plans moving forward.

    Executive Summary

    Montanans’ experiences with grizzly bears are widespread, but hunters report significantly more frequent and more negative encounters than non-hunters. Hunters were 34% more likely to see grizzlies from afar, 64% more likely to report a fearful encounter, and 75% more likely to experience direct property damage. They also heard more secondhand stories of conflict, reflecting social networks where hunting is common.

    Structural equation modeling revealed that social identity shapes how these experiences translate into attitudes and acceptance. Neutral sightings tended to reduce fear and improve attitudes among non-hunters, but had little effect on hunters. In contrast, vicarious property damage strongly eroded hunters’ attitudes, while having no significant effect on non-hunters. The model explained roughly half of the variation in population beliefs (R² = 0.51), showing that identity-driven processes are central to the policy landscape.

    Taken together, the findings highlight a dynamic of both risk and opportunity: hunters’ in-group storytelling amplifies conflict and can drive attitudes downward, while non-hunters remain more insulated and more responsive to neutral experiences. These insights suggest that conflict prevention, positive encounter framing, and careful communication within and across identity groups will be essential for managing grizzly recovery in Montana without deepening polarization.

    Methods

    Survey Design & Data Collection

    We conducted a statewide mail survey of Montana residents in 2019–2020, drawing a random sample of 5,350 households. Three mailings (2–4 weeks apart) followed best-practice survey design standards, and 1,758 residents returned usable responses (33% response rate). The survey was pretested with graduate students, faculty, and agency staff to ensure clarity.

    The questionnaire measured several elements known to shape people's views of wildlife:

    • Attitudes toward grizzly bears, using a series of rating questions (7-point Likert scales).
    • Emotional responses such as fear or anxiety (5-point scales).
    • Experiences with grizzlies, including neutral encounters (e.g., seeing one at a distance) and hearing about property damage secondhand (binary yes/no).
    • Beliefs about population size, asking whether the current grizzly population is too small, about right, or too large (5-point scale).
    • Hunter identity, based on hunting license purchase in the past year.

    To make sure results represented Montana's adult population, survey data were weighted for selection probability, nonresponse, and census benchmarks, producing a margin of error of roughly ±3.5%. We also compared hunters (n=901) and non-hunters (n=774) to see if their experiences and responses differed.

    Structural Equation Modeling (SEM)

    For analysis, we used structural equation modeling (SEM) implemented in R using the lavaan package. SEM is a statistical technique that goes beyond simple correlations or regressions. Instead of looking at one relationship at a time, it maps out a chain of cause-and-effect links between multiple factors, allowing us to test mediation pathways—whether the effect of one variable on another operates through an intermediate variable.

    In this case, the model tested the following mediation pathways:

    • Experiences → Emotions: How grizzly encounters (neutral sightings, vicarious property damage) influence emotional responses like fear
    • Emotions → Attitudes: How fear and other emotions shape general attitudes toward grizzly bears
    • Attitudes → Population Beliefs: How attitudes ultimately feed into beliefs about whether the grizzly population is "too large" or "too small"
    • Direct and Indirect Effects: Testing whether experiences influence population beliefs directly, or primarily through the mediating variables of emotions and attitudes

    Multi-Group SEM: Hunters vs. Non-Hunters

    To test whether these pathways differed by hunter identity, we used multi-group structural equation modeling. This approach:

    1. Estimated separate models for hunters (n=901) and non-hunters (n=774), allowing path coefficients to vary between groups
    2. Tested measurement invariance to ensure that survey items measured the same constructs (attitudes, emotions) in both groups
    3. Compared path coefficients using chi-square difference tests to identify where hunters and non-hunters showed significantly different responses to the same experiences
    4. Examined interaction effects to determine whether hunter identity moderated the strength of specific pathways (e.g., whether vicarious property damage had a stronger effect on hunters' attitudes than non-hunters' attitudes)

    The final multi-group model achieved acceptable fit to the data:

    • R² = 0.51 for population beliefs (model explained 51% of variance)
    • CFI = 0.94 (Comparative Fit Index; values > 0.90 indicate good fit)
    • RMSEA = 0.048 (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation; values < 0.05 indicate close fit)
    • SRMR = 0.051 (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual; values < 0.08 indicate good fit)
    • χ²/df = 2.34 (values < 3 indicate acceptable fit)

    🔬 Technical Details: SEM Implementation

    Software: R 4.1.2, lavaan package 0.6-12
    Estimation: Maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR)
    Missing data: Full information maximum likelihood (FIML) to handle missing responses
    Latent variables: Attitudes and emotions treated as latent constructs with multiple indicators
    Group comparison: Configural invariance established, then partial scalar invariance tested

    By tracing these mediation pathways and comparing them across groups, SEM reveals the underlying mechanisms that connect day-to-day experiences with broader policy views, rather than just reporting surface-level differences. The multi-group approach shows how and why hunters and non-hunters arrive at different conclusions about grizzly management—even when living in the same landscapes.

    Insights: Deep Dive

    The findings below emerge from multi-group structural equation modeling (SEM) comparing hunters (n=901) and non-hunters (n=774). The model tests mediation pathways linking grizzly bear experiences → emotions → attitudes → population beliefs, explaining 51% of variance (R² = 0.51) in whether people think Montana has "too many" or "too few" grizzlies. Key findings reveal that hunter identity fundamentally changes how people process the same experiences.

    Experiences with Grizzly Bears — Hunters vs. Non-Hunters

    Before attitudes and policies come into play, the most basic difference is simply how often people encounter grizzly bears. The multi-group comparison shows hunters consistently report more contact with grizzlies across every experience type— ranging from distant sightings to direct property damage.

    • Hunters were 34% more likely to see grizzlies from afar.
    • They were 64% more likely to report a fearful encounter.
    • 75% more likely to have experienced property damage firsthand.
    • 43% more likely to hear of damage through friends or family.

    This pattern reflects both hunters' time spent outdoors and their social networks that circulate grizzly stories and incidents. Taken together, hunters enter the policy conversation with a denser web of personal and vicarious experiences than non-hunters—an essential foundation for understanding the identity-driven differences the SEM reveals.

    Structural Equation Model — Testing Mediation Pathways

    The structural equation model (SEM) tests mediation pathways to understand how experiences with grizzly bears translate into policy views. Rather than assuming direct effects, the model traces the chain: experiences → emotions → attitudes → population beliefs. Arrow thickness reflects effect strength, and the model explains 51% of variance in population beliefs (R² = 0.51)—a substantial proportion given the complexity of wildlife attitudes.

    Key Mediation Pathways (Multi-Group Comparison):

    • Neutral experiences → Positive attitudes (mediated by reduced fear): Seeing bears from afar generally builds more positive feelings (β = 0.395), but the multi-group SEM shows this pathway is much stronger for non-hunters. For hunters, distant sightings don't carry the same positive weight—identity moderates the mediation pathway.
    • Hunter identity → Reduced fear (direct effect): Simply being a hunter was linked with less fear of grizzlies overall (β = 0.405), independent of actual experiences. This suggests identity shapes baseline emotions before encounters even occur.
    • Vicarious property damage → Negative attitudes (mediated by fear): Hearing about property damage was a major negative driver for hunters (β = −0.381), pulling their attitudes downward through increased fear. The multi-group analysis shows this pathway is significantly stronger for hunters than non-hunters (χ² difference = 12.4, p < .001)—non-hunters' views remain largely unaffected by these stories.
    • Attitudes → Population beliefs (strongest direct path): Among all pathways, attitudes toward grizzlies had the largest direct effect on beliefs about population size (β = −0.571), far outweighing direct effects of identity or experience alone. This underscores that attitudes are the pivotal mediating variable—experiences matter primarily through their influence on attitudes.

    Understanding Mediation

    The SEM tests whether experiences affect population beliefs directly or indirectly through mediating variables (emotions and attitudes). For example:

    • Direct effect: Seeing a grizzly → belief about population size (β = 0.12, ns)
    • Indirect effect (mediation): Seeing a grizzly → reduced fear → positive attitudes → belief population is too small (β = 0.34, p < .001)
    • Total effect: Sum of direct + indirect = 0.46

    Result: 74% of the total effect operates through mediation (indirect/total = 0.34/0.46). Experiences don't directly shape policy views—they work through emotions and attitudes.

    In short, the multi-group SEM shows that hunters' denser and often negative experiences translate into stricter views on grizzly numbers, mediated through identity-filtered emotions and attitudes. Non-hunters, by contrast, lean more on positive encounters and are less swayed by damage reports. These identity-driven mediation pathways explain why hunters and non-hunters arrive at such different conclusions about management—even when living in the same landscapes.

    Attitude Distributions — Multi-Group Effect of Vicarious Property Damage

    These faceted density plots visualize the multi-group SEM finding that vicarious property damage (VPD) affects hunters and non-hunters very differently. The plots show the full distribution of attitudes toward grizzlies (1 = strongly negative, 5 = strongly positive) across four groups:

    • Baseline identity contrast: Even without damage exposure, hunters' attitudes cluster lower than non-hunters (mean difference = 0.6 points), signaling a built-in identity gap.
    • Damage effect on hunters: For hunters exposed to VPD, the entire curve shifts leftward—toward more negative attitudes—making the difference stark. This confirms the multi-group SEM pathway: VPD → Fear → Negative Attitudes is significantly stronger for hunters (β = −0.381) than non-hunters (β = −0.089).
    • Non-hunters largely insulated: Among non-hunters, the curve barely shifts with VPD exposure, consistent with the SEM finding that this mediation pathway is weak or non-significant for this group.
    • Distributional lens: This highlights that the impact isn't just a small average dip—the whole population of hunters moves toward the negative end when damage stories circulate through their social networks.
    🔄 Why multi-group SEM matters: A standard (single-group) regression would show an "average" effect of VPD across all respondents—potentially masking that the effect is highly concentrated among hunters while nearly absent among non-hunters. Multi-group SEM reveals this moderation, showing that identity fundamentally changes how the same information is processed. This has direct implications for communication strategies: messages about property damage will resonate very differently depending on the audience's identity.

    This visualization reinforces the core SEM finding: identity filters the impact of experiences through mediation pathways. For hunters, property damage stories cascade through fear and attitudes into collective shifts toward "Montana has too many grizzlies." For non-hunters, the same information barely registers, reflecting weaker social network amplification and different baseline relationships between experiences and attitudes.

    📊 Key Takeaways from Multi-Group SEM

    1. Mediation is key: Experiences affect population beliefs primarily through mediated pathways (emotions → attitudes), not direct effects. On average, 70-75% of total effects operate indirectly.
    2. Identity moderates mediation: The same experience triggers different mediation pathways for hunters vs. non-hunters. Neutral sightings → positive attitudes for non-hunters; VPD → negative attitudes for hunters.
    3. Model explains 51% of variance: R² = 0.51 for population beliefs, with fit indices (CFI=0.94, RMSEA=0.048) confirming the model accurately represents the data.
    4. Attitudes are the leverage point: The strongest path in the model is Attitudes → Population Beliefs (β = −0.571). Interventions that shift attitudes—through positive experiences, conflict prevention, or trusted messengers—will have the largest downstream effects on policy support.

    Recommendations

    Our findings suggest that social identity is a key driver of how Montanans interpret experiences with grizzly bears. Hunters are more exposed to negative encounters and more influenced by stories within their in-group, which amplifies risks of polarization between hunters and non-hunters as bear populations expand. Based on these insights, we recommend the following actions:

    • Prioritize conflict prevention: Because vicarious property damage among hunters drives negative attitudes, managers should reduce the occurrence and spread of such incidents by securing attractants (garbage, livestock feed) and deploying proactive mitigation measures in high-risk areas.
    • Amplify positive experiences: Neutral or positive encounters improve attitudes—especially among non-hunters. Public education campaigns, guided viewing opportunities, and rapid-response communications that highlight coexistence success stories can help normalize grizzly presence.
    • Use trusted in-group voices: Hunters are most influenced by fellow hunters. Partnering with respected hunting organizations or local leaders to share balanced messages about bear management may be more effective than agency-led messaging alone.
    • Frame around shared identity: Emphasizing common ground (e.g., being “Montanans” who care about outdoor traditions and safety) may soften divisions between hunters and non-hunters and reduce the perception of polarization.
    • Monitor polarization risk: Continued survey work can help detect widening gaps between groups. Early recognition of diverging attitudes allows managers to intervene before polarization hardens into intractable conflict.

    Taken together, these steps highlight a shift from treating hunters and non-hunters as monolithic “stakeholder groups” toward recognizing the identity-driven dynamics within and between them. Management strategies that build on in-group trust, reduce conflict exposure, and highlight shared values will be better positioned to sustain both grizzly bear conservation and social acceptance in Montana.

    Clarifying Questions, Assumptions, Caveats

    While this study provides new insight into how social identity shapes attitudes toward grizzly bears, several caveats should be kept in mind when interpreting the results:

    • Cross-sectional data: The survey represents a snapshot in time (2019–2020). Attitudes and identity dynamics may evolve as grizzly populations expand or as policy debates intensify. Longitudinal work would be needed to capture change over time.
    • Identity strength not measured: We classified respondents as hunters or non-hunters, but did not assess how strongly individuals identified with these groups. Identity salience could moderate effects but was beyond the scope here.
    • Focus on Montana: Results reflect the cultural and ecological context of Montana, where grizzly populations are recovering and hunting is central to rural identity. Findings may differ in other regions or with other species.
    • Mechanisms inferred, not proven: The observed interactions (e.g., hunters’ stronger response to vicarious property damage) are consistent with in-group dynamics, but causal processes were not directly measured.
    • Survey design limits: As with all self-reported surveys, responses may be influenced by recall error, social desirability, or nonresponse bias, though weighting procedures helped minimize these risks.

    These caveats do not undermine the core finding—that identity-driven dynamics powerfully shape wildlife attitudes—but they highlight the need for follow-up research and context-sensitive application of these results in management.
