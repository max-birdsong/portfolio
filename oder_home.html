OderTogether Survey Framework

Integrated data collection across resident, angler, and catch log surveys

The OderTogether research project uses three complementary survey instruments to assess ecosystem services, recreational fishing values, and fish population status following the 2022 Oder River ecological incident. Each instrument targets different stakeholder groups and captures distinct but overlapping data dimensions. All instruments follow cutting-edge methodological standards from the field's most recent synthesis (Pope et al. 2026).

Methodological Standards

All survey design follows best practices from Pope et al. (2026) "Understanding Recreational Fishers"

✓ Validated Scales

Use established, psychometrically-tested instruments from Kyle et al. (2026) compendium rather than ad hoc items. Specialization measured via validated centrality scale; satisfaction uses construct-specific measures (Birdsong et al. 2021).

✓ Optimal Scale Length

5-point Likert scales for all constructs (Revilla et al. 2014). Higher numbers consistently indicate more positive responses. Bipolar scales state both sides ("oppose or support") to reduce social desirability bias (Vaske et al. 2026).

✓ Professional Translation

TRAPD protocol (Translation, Review, Adjudication, Pretesting, Documentation) for German-Polish equivalence. Professional translators with back-translation verification and cognitive interviews with native speakers (Harkness et al. 2010).

✓ Sample Size Requirements

Creel survey: 30-40 days minimum for 20% relative standard error in degraded fishery (Lester et al. 1991; McCormick et al. 2015). ES survey: n=1,200 for cross-country factor analysis with adequate statistical power.

✓ Bias Mitigation

Track repeat encounters ("Interviewed before this season?") to address avidity bias in on-site creel (de Kerckhove et al. 2026). Use Model II regression for temporal extrapolation when intercepting mid-trip (Alexiades et al. 2015).

✓ Rigorous Pilot Testing

Three-stage process: (1) Expert review by human dimensions researchers, (2) Cognitive interviews (n=10 per language, think-aloud protocol), (3) Pilot deployment with preliminary factor analysis and reliability testing (Cronbach's α ≥ 0.70).

These standards ensure: (1) data quality and measurement validity, (2) cross-study comparability, (3) methodological defensibility under peer review, and (4) alignment with IGB research tradition established by Kochalski et al. (2022) salmon ES study.

Survey Instrument Overview

Resident Ecosystem Services Survey
Duration: 10 minutes
Mode: Online panel
Languages: DE/PL/EN
A. Connection to Oder
Distance, visit frequency, activities, quality of life importance
B. ES Importance (14 items)
Water quality, flood protection, biodiversity, fish, recreation, aesthetics, well-being, culture, education, economic, fishing, climate, sense of place, future generations
C. Priority & Tradeoff
Top 3 priorities + budget allocation (100 points)
D. 2022 Event Awareness
Awareness, concern, management priorities
E. Stakeholders & Trust
Who should be involved, trust ratings
F. Demographics
Age, gender, education, angler status, national identity
Methodological foundation: Survey design builds on established IGB approach from multi-country ES research (Kochalski et al. 2022) and applies current best practices from Pope et al. (2026). Uses 5-point Likert scales for importance ratings (optimal scale length per Revilla et al. 2014), exploratory factor analysis to group ES into meaningful categories, and examines how personal characteristics, values, and ecological context shape ES perception. Content analysis of open-ended responses follows Kochalski et al. (2026) guidance with theoretical saturation (~80-100 responses) and inter-coder reliability testing (Cohen's Kappa ≥0.70). Question construction follows Vaske et al. (2026) guidelines: simple language, defined terms, construct-specific scales, both sides stated ("oppose or support"), demographics at end. Key insight: ES valuation is "co-produced by ecology and society"—ecological recovery can foster development of perceived ES (Kochalski et al. 2022).
Angler Creel Module
Duration: 5-7 minutes
Mode: Riverside intercept
Languages: DE/PL*
Today's Fishing Trip
Motivation, harvest intention, satisfaction
Catch & Effort Data
Start time, target species, fish caught, fish kept, trip completion status
Angler Background
Years fishing, trips per year, skill level, psychological commitment
Context & Attitudes
Crowding perception, stocking importance
🎤 INTERVIEW KEY - Creel Design: Survey follows best practices from de Kerckhove et al. (2026) chapter on optimizing creel surveys. Sample size: 30-40 survey days minimum for 20% relative standard error in degraded post-disaster fishery (Lester et al. 1991; McCormick et al. 2015 provide exact formulas). Specialization measurement: We measure centrality (psychological commitment) specifically—NOT full "specialization" which requires 3 dimensions (Landon et al. 2026). Centrality predicts conservation-oriented behavior (C&R) during ecological stress (Sutton & Ditton 2001). Avidity bias: Track repeat encounters ("Interviewed before this season?") because on-site sampling over-represents avid anglers even when random—70% of anglers sampled only once in 14 years (Cowie & Ridgway 2023). We weight by encounter probability. Temporal bias: Record expected trip end time; use Model II regression for extrapolation when intercepting mid-trip (Alexiades et al. 2015). Key terms defined: "fishing day", "trip", "catch", "harvest" all operationally defined to avoid ambiguity (Vaske et al. 2026 guideline #12). Scale format: 5-point Likert throughout (optimal length per Revilla et al. 2014); construct-specific scales (satisfaction = dissatisfied/satisfied, NOT agree/disagree). Interview takes 5-7 minutes to minimize burden and maximize cooperation rates.
Catch Log Study
Duration: Per trip / Season
Mode: Paper/Online log
Languages: DE
Background (One-time)
Years fishing, annual days, club membership, permit type, purchase method
Per-Trip Logging
Date, time, location, expenses, target species, fishing method, catch success
Catch Details
Species, length (≥20cm), harvest decision for each fish
Additional Measures
Satisfaction, target species preferences, equipment value, hobby importance, fishing style preferences
🎤 INTERVIEW KEY - Catch Logs: Self-reported data prone to biases (recall, prestige, species misidentification—Essig & Holliday 1991), but catch logs provide revealed preferences to validate stated preferences from ES survey. Bias reduction strategies: (1) Rapid reporting encouraged (same-day or next-day logging reduces recall bias), (2) Smartphone app minimizes data entry burden but has avidity bias—most engaged anglers participate (Venturelli et al. 2017, 2026), (3) Paper logbooks offered as alternative to expand demographic reach. Key insight from meta-analysis (Birdsong et al. 2021—cited throughout Pope et al. 2026): Catch-satisfaction relationships are context-dependent and moderated by specialization. Specialized anglers show weaker catch-satisfaction links than casual anglers. Post-disaster Oder provides unique context to test whether ecological stress changes these relationships. Validation approach: Compare catch log participation rates and catch metrics against on-site creel survey to assess selection bias and develop correction factors if needed.

Data Collection Flow

1
Instrument Development & Translation 🎤
INTERVIEW KEY: Survey design uses validated scales from Kyle et al. (2026) compendium (DOI: 10.18738/T8/LFWX64) rather than ad hoc items. Professional German-Polish translation follows TRAPD protocol (Translation, Review, Adjudication, Pretesting, Documentation—Harkness et al. 2010). Cognitive interviews with native speakers (n=10 per language) use think-aloud protocol to identify confusing items. Pilot deployment (n=50 per side) with preliminary factor analysis (KMO ≥0.80, Bartlett's p≤0.05) and reliability testing (Cronbach's α ≥0.70). Three-stage pilot process ensures cross-cultural equivalence and measurement validity before main deployment.
2
Resident Survey Deployment
Online panel recruitment with quotas (age, gender, distance), 12-week data collection window, quality monitoring
3
Angler Intercept Surveys (Creel) 🎤
INTERVIEW KEY: Stratified sampling across weekday/weekend and AM/PM periods. Minimum 30-40 survey days for 20% RSE (Lester et al. 1991; McCormick et al. 2015 formulas). Focus on 5-10 major access points per region (Pareto principle: 80% of anglers at 20% of sites). Track repeat encounters to address avidity bias: "Have you been interviewed this season?" Weight analyses by encounter probability because 70% of anglers sampled only once in 14 years (Cowie & Ridgway 2023). Record start time, interview time, expected end time for temporal bias correction (Model II regression). Mobile-optimized survey keeps interviews <8 minutes to maximize cooperation rates. Roving creel design (not just access point) provides better spatial coverage.
4
Catch Log Registration & Distribution
Annual permit holders register online or by phone, receive paper logbook or online access code, season-long documentation
5
Data Integration & Analysis 🎤
INTERVIEW KEY: Multi-method validation: stated preferences (ES survey) vs. revealed preferences (catch logs) to assess convergent validity. Content analysis of open-ended responses follows Kochalski et al. (2026) with theoretical saturation (~80-100 responses), hybrid deductive-inductive coding, and inter-coder reliability (Cohen's Kappa ≥0.70). Cross-country comparisons use measurement invariance testing (configural, metric, scalar) to verify German-Polish comparability before substantive analysis. Factor analysis groups ES items into meaningful categories (KMO ≥0.80). Creel data weighted by encounter probability to correct avidity bias. Satisfaction models test whether meta-analytic findings (Birdsong et al. 2021) hold in post-disaster context. Integration enables triangulation: Do people who say fish are important (stated) actually fish more (revealed)? Do ES priorities predict management support?

Integration Strategy

The three instruments are designed to provide complementary perspectives on the Oder River system:

*Angler creel survey designed for flexible deployment. Can be implemented German-side only or extended to Polish side depending on project resources, partnerships, and permitting. Survey instrument includes DE/PL translations to enable cross-border deployment if feasible.

Resident Survey captures broad public values across the full ES portfolio, trust in governance, and awareness of the 2022 incident. This provides the population-level baseline for ES priorities and identifies which services matter most to different user groups.

Angler Creel Module provides deeper insight into a key stakeholder group (anglers) through field-based data collection. Captures motivation, specialization, value priorities, and satisfaction in situ, allowing comparison between stated preferences (resident survey) and revealed preferences (actual fishing behavior).

Catch Log Study generates objective biological data (catch rates, species composition, size structure) through citizen science. Links angler characteristics and preferences to actual fish population status, enabling assessment of both ecological recovery and recreational fishing quality.

Publication Strategy

While the full OderTogether project integrates across all three instruments, the existing OderAngeln catch log data enables immediate publication opportunities. A focused 3-paper strategy addresses recovery dynamics, angler psychology, and adaptive behavior using catch log data alone, demonstrating analytical capacity and establishing baseline understanding of recreational fishing on the Oder River post-2022 disaster.

Stakeholder Mapping & Workshop Design

Survey data informs stakeholder engagement by identifying key actor groups, their priorities, and trust relationships. The resident survey explicitly asks who should be involved in decision-making (Q12) and measures trust in different stakeholder groups (Q13), providing an empirical foundation for workshop design.

Stakeholder Role Mapping

Based on survey responses, stakeholders can be categorized by:

  • → Legitimacy: Who residents believe should be involved (local residents, anglers, NGOs, municipal government, national agencies, scientists, industry, tourism)
  • → Trust level: How much confidence residents have in each group to act in the river's long-term interest
  • → Value priorities: What different groups prioritize (ES importance ratings, management priorities, tradeoff preferences)
  • → Cross-border differences: How German vs Polish residents differ in priorities and trust

Transboundary Workshop Structure

Survey outputs can structure interactive workshops around shared and contested priorities:

  • → Common ground identification: Which ES are universally valued across groups and borders? (e.g., water quality, biodiversity)
  • → Conflict mapping: Where do priorities diverge? (e.g., flood protection vs habitat restoration, economic use vs conservation)
  • → Tradeoff negotiation: Budget allocation data reveals how people make hard choices when resources are limited
  • → Trust-building pathways: Understanding which groups are trusted (or not) suggests who should lead different initiatives

Workshop activities could include collaborative mapping exercises where stakeholders visualize priority areas, scenario planning based on survey tradeoff data, and structured dialogue around survey findings that highlight both consensus and divergence. The cross-border component requires careful facilitation to ensure Polish and German perspectives are equally represented and language barriers are addressed.

Synthesis: Theoretical Positioning

The OderTogether project sits at the intersection of multiple literatures:

  • → Ecosystem services valuation — Applying established frameworks (MAES, IPBES) to a transboundary river system
  • → Relational values — Expanding beyond instrumental use to capture place attachment, identity, and cultural meaning
  • → Environmental disturbance impacts — Drawing on HAB and fisheries closure literature to understand social consequences of the 2022 disaster
  • → Cross-border governance — Informing transboundary management through empirical understanding of value differences and trust dynamics
  • → Human dimensions of fisheries — Integrating angler perspectives, motivations, and satisfaction into broader ES framework

By combining these threads, the research generates actionable knowledge for sustainable, socially-legitimate Oder River management while contributing methodological advances in cultural ES assessment and cross-border natural resource governance.
