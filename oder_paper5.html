Transboundary Ecosystem Services: How Shared Rivers Create Unshared Values in the Post-Disaster Oder Basin

Target Journals: Ecosystem Services, Environmental Science & Policy, Global Environmental Change

🎤 INTERVIEW KEY - Synthesis Paper

  • Co-production framework (Kochalski et al. 2022): "ES perception is co-produced by ecology and society"—we test with same ecology, different society. Natural experiment design.
  • Measurement invariance (NEW): Before comparing means across countries, must test configural, metric, and scalar invariance. Ensures German/Polish respondents interpret items same way. Kyle et al. (2026) psychometric standards.
  • Multiple elicitation methods: Importance ratings + Top 3 ranking + budget allocation. Tests whether differences are robust across methods (convergent validity).
  • Stated vs. revealed preferences: If Polish catch log data available, can validate stated priorities against actual fishing behavior. Do people who say fish are important actually fish more?
  • Arlinghaus co-production framework: Knowledge co-production chapter (Pope et al. 2026) provides stakeholder workshop design. German-Polish workshops need neutral facilitator, power balance attention, "blue fear" mitigation.
  • Policy relevance: Transboundary coordination requires understanding WHERE values align (build on common ground) and WHERE they diverge (negotiate tradeoffs).

Abstract

Transboundary rivers require coordinated management, yet riparian nations often hold divergent priorities for ecosystem services (ES). Following the catastrophic 2022 Oder River fish kill that affected both German and Polish territories, we examine how residents of each nation value the river's ES three years post-disaster. Using survey data from N=1,200 residents (600 per country), we find significant cross-border differences in ES priorities despite shared ecological conditions. Polish residents rate extractive-provisioning ES (fishing, economic benefits) [X]% higher than Germans (p<0.001, Cohen's d=[TBD]), while Germans rate intrinsic-ecological ES (biodiversity, future generations) [Y]% higher. These differences persist after controlling for demographics, river proximity, and environmental values, indicating genuine cultural divergence rather than compositional artifacts. Decomposition analysis reveals that [Z]% of the German-Polish gap is explained by differences in population characteristics, with [remaining]% attributable to cultural-institutional factors including post-socialist vs. Western governance traditions, economic development levels, and differential experience with extractive fisheries. Disaster awareness heightens ES valuation in both countries, but strengthens extractive ES importance more in Poland and intrinsic ES importance more in Germany, amplifying rather than bridging cross-border differences. Findings demonstrate that ES valuation is co-produced not only by ecological conditions and personal experiences but also by national cultural contexts that shape how people conceptualize human-nature relationships. Management implications emphasize the need for transboundary coordination strategies that acknowledge divergent value priorities while building on shared recognition of core ES (water quality, flood protection) to establish common ground for collaborative governance.

Introduction

Transboundary Water Governance Challenges

Over 260 river basins cross national boundaries, serving more than 40% of the world's population and generating persistent governance challenges (UNESCO 2009, Schmeier 2013). Effective transboundary water management requires coordination among riparian nations that may differ in political systems, economic development, environmental priorities, and cultural relationships with rivers. The European Union's Water Framework Directive (2000) mandates coordinated management of shared water bodies, yet implementation reveals persistent tensions between member states over priorities, standards, and resource allocation.

A fundamental challenge is that nations sharing the same river may not share the same values for its ecosystem services. While all parties may agree abstractly that rivers provide multiple benefits, disagreements emerge when tradeoffs are necessary: Should water be allocated for agricultural production or ecological flows? Should fisheries be managed for economic extraction or biodiversity conservation? Should river infrastructure prioritize flood control or natural habitat? These conflicts reflect not only competing interests but potentially divergent fundamental values about human-nature relationships.

Ecosystem Services in Cross-Cultural Context

The ecosystem services framework posits that nature provides benefits to humanity, categorized as provisioning (food, water), regulating (flood control, climate), supporting (nutrient cycling, habitat), and cultural services (recreation, spiritual, aesthetic) (MEA 2005). However, which services are recognized, valued, and prioritized varies substantially across cultural contexts (Chan et al. 2012, Kochalski et al. 2022). Cross-country ES research consistently finds both universal patterns (widespread support for water quality, flood protection) and context-specific priorities reflecting local needs, traditions, and environmental histories.

ES valuation is fundamentally a co-production process (Chan et al. 2012; Kochalski et al. 2022): ecosystem conditions provide potential services, but recognition and valuation depend on cultural frameworks, experiential interactions, economic conditions, and institutional contexts. 🎤 KEY FINDING from Kochalski et al. (2022) salmon study: Countries with healthier ecological conditions (Norway) showed higher ES valuation across ALL categories compared to countries with degraded populations (France, Germany, Sweden). This demonstrates that "improving ecological status can foster development of perceived ecosystem services"—ES perception and ecosystem health are mutually reinforcing. Our Oder study tests this in reverse: Does ecological degradation (2022 disaster) reduce ES perception? And does it affect German vs. Polish residents differently given their different baseline ecological conditions and cultural contexts?

The German-Polish Oder Context

The Oder River forms much of the German-Polish border, flowing through both nations before reaching the Baltic Sea. The 2022 environmental disaster—caused by toxic golden algae that killed over 300 tons of fish across 500 km—affected German and Polish territories simultaneously and with similar ecological severity. This creates a rare natural experiment: both nations experienced the identical ecological shock to the same river, yet they differ substantially in:

  • Economic development: Germany's GDP per capita (~$51,000) is 2.5× Poland's (~$20,000), affecting environment-economy tradeoff priorities
  • Political systems: Western liberal democracy with strong Green movement (Germany) vs. post-socialist democratic transition with development emphasis (Poland)
  • Ecological conditions: German Oder sections have highly degraded fish populations with local extinctions; Polish sections retain more viable fisheries with active harvest
  • Fishing culture: German romantic/sport angling tradition vs. Polish subsistence/commercial fishing heritage
  • Environmental governance: Decades of strict EU environmental regulation in former West Germany vs. relatively recent EU accession (2004) and ongoing development priorities in Poland

These differences provide leverage to test three types of explanations for cross-border ES divergence: ecological (different abilities to use extractive services), economic (development level affects priorities), and cultural-institutional (different environmental governance traditions and values).

Research Gap

While many studies compare ES priorities across countries (Kochalski et al. 2022, Riepe et al. 2019), few examine transboundary systems where riparian nations share the exact same ecological conditions. Most cross-country comparisons confound national culture with different ecosystems, making it impossible to isolate whether differences reflect values or ecology. The Oder case controls for ecological conditions (same river, same disaster) while varying cultural-institutional contexts, enabling clearer identification of cultural effects on ES valuation. Furthermore, most cross-country ES research examines stable systems; the post-disaster context tests whether ecological crises amplify or reduce cross-cultural differences in priorities.

Research Questions

  1. RQ1: Which ecosystem services show the largest German-Polish differences? Do differences persist across multiple elicitation methods (importance ratings, Top 3 priorities, budget allocation)?
  2. RQ2: Are cross-border differences attributable to population composition (demographics, proximity, values) or genuine cultural-institutional divergence?
  3. RQ3: How does disaster awareness affect ES valuation, and does it amplify or reduce cross-border differences?
  4. RQ4: Do Germans and Poles differ in stakeholder trust and preferred governance approaches, and do these differences correlate with ES priorities?
  5. RQ5: Among anglers specifically (if Polish catch log data available): Do cross-border differences in stated ES priorities predict differences in revealed preferences (fishing behavior, satisfaction, harvest)?

Conceptual Framework

Three-Layer Model of Cross-Border ES Divergence

Layer 1: Ecological Context (Same River, Different Access)

  • 2022 disaster reduced fish populations equally on both sides of border
  • BUT: Baseline conditions differ—Polish sections have healthier pre-disaster populations, active fisheries
  • German sections already heavily degraded, wild fish locally extinct in many areas
  • Prediction: Poles value extractive ES more because they can actually USE them

Layer 2: Economic Development (Post-Industrial vs. Developing)

  • Germany: Post-industrial society with leisure-oriented environmentalism
  • Poland: Still industrializing, economic growth priorities compete with environmental protection
  • Environmental Kuznets Curve logic: wealthy societies prioritize environmental quality over economic extraction
  • Prediction: Germans value aesthetic/intrinsic ES; Poles value economic/extractive ES

Layer 3: Cultural-Institutional (Historical Legacies)

  • Germany: Decades of Green Party influence, strong environmental movement, strict regulations
  • Poland: Post-socialist transition, lower institutional trust, weaker environmental civil society
  • Different fisheries management traditions: German Romanticism (nature as spiritual refuge) vs. Polish subsistence heritage
  • Prediction: Cultural differences persist even after controlling for ecology and economics

↓ Co-Production of ES Valuation ↓

Resulting ES Priorities:

  • Universal convergence on core ES (water, flood)
  • Divergence on extractive ES (economic, fishing) - Poland higher
  • Divergence on intrinsic ES (biodiversity, future generations) - Germany higher
  • Moderate divergence on cultural ES (aesthetics, well-being)

Hypotheses

H1: Extractive ES Gap

H1a: Polish residents will rate "Fishing as a local activity" and "Economic benefits (tourism, local business)" 30-50% higher than German residents.

H1b: This gap will be largest among respondents living close to the river (<20 km), where fishing access is most salient.

H1c: The gap will persist after controlling for demographics, angler status, and biospheric values, indicating it reflects genuine cultural difference rather than composition effects.

H2: Intrinsic-Ecological Convergence (with German Advantage)

H2a: Both nations will rate water quality, flood protection, and biodiversity as highly important (means >4.0/5.0), showing universal recognition of core ES.

H2b: Germans will rate "Maintaining healthy fish populations," "Option value: future generations," and "Sense of place" modestly higher (10-20%) than Poles, reflecting stronger intrinsic environmental values.

H2c: Education will predict intrinsic ES valuation more strongly in Germany than Poland (interaction effect), consistent with environmental literacy narratives.

H3: Cultural ES - Moderate Divergence

H3: Germans will rate aesthetic and well-being ES modestly higher (15-25%) than Poles, reflecting post-industrial leisure orientation. Poles will rate cultural heritage/identity ES higher, reflecting stronger place attachment in less-mobile population.

H4: Disaster Awareness Amplifies Differences

H4a: High disaster awareness will increase ES valuation in both countries, but differentially: awareness will strengthen extractive ES importance in Poland (highlighting lost livelihoods) and intrinsic ES importance in Germany (highlighting biodiversity crisis).

H4b: Cross-border differences in ES priorities will be LARGER among high-awareness respondents than low-awareness respondents, indicating disaster interpretation differs culturally.

H5: Decomposition of Country Effects

H5a: Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition will show that 40-60% of German-Polish ES priority differences are explained by compositional differences (age, education, income, proximity, values), with 40-60% attributable to cultural-institutional factors.

H5b: Unexplained ("cultural") portion will be largest for extractive and heritage ES, smallest for regulating ES.

H6: Governance Preferences

H6a: Germans will express higher trust in government and scientific institutions than Poles, consistent with institutional trust literature.

H6b: Germans will prefer NGO involvement in management; Poles will prefer government/industry leadership, reflecting different civil society traditions.

H6c: Trust in institutions will mediate relationship between country and intrinsic ES valuation: Germans trust institutions → support regulatory ES more.

Data & Methods

Primary Data Source

Resident Ecosystem Services Survey (Paper 4 data)

  • N = 1,200 residents (600 Germany, 600 Poland)
  • 14 ES importance items (5-point scale)
  • Top 3 ES priorities (forced ranking)
  • Budget allocation (100 points across ES categories)
  • Disaster awareness and concern
  • Stakeholder trust ratings (Section E)
  • Demographics, values, river connection (Section F)

Secondary Data (If Available)

Catch Log Study (Papers 1-3 data)

Analytical Approach

Step 1: Descriptive Cross-Border Comparison

Importance Ratings:

  • Calculate mean and SD for each of 14 ES items by country
  • Independent t-tests with Bonferroni correction (14 comparisons, α = 0.0036)
  • Effect sizes (Cohen's d) to identify substantively meaningful differences
  • Diverging bar chart showing German-Polish differences for each ES

Top 3 Priorities:

  • Calculate selection frequency for each ES by country
  • Chi-square tests for independence
  • Identify which ES are "important but not prioritized" vs. "prioritized despite moderate importance" in each country

Budget Allocation:

  • Mean points allocated to each ES category by country
  • Test whether cross-border differences are larger in budget allocation than importance ratings (forced tradeoffs reveal priorities more clearly)

Step 2: Blinder-Oaxaca Decomposition

For each ES dimension (from factor analysis), decompose German-Polish mean difference into:

  • Explained component: Difference due to compositional effects (Germans are older, more educated, live further from river, etc.)
  • Unexplained component: Difference due to coefficients (same characteristics predict different ES values in each country) = "cultural" effect

Model:

ES_importance = β₀ + β₁(Age) + β₂(Education) + β₃(Income) + β₄(Urban) + β₅(Distance) + β₆(BiosphericValues) + β₇(Angler) + ε

Estimate separately for Germany and Poland, then decompose mean difference.

Step 3: Multilevel Models with Country Interactions

Test whether predictors of ES valuation differ by country:

Model:

ES_factor_score = β₀ + β₁(Country) + β₂(X) + β₃(Country × X) + controls

Where X includes: Biospheric values, Education, Proximity, Angler status, Disaster awareness

Significant Country × X interactions indicate that relationships differ across border (e.g., education predicts intrinsic ES more strongly in Germany).

Step 4: Disaster Awareness Analysis

  • Create high/low awareness groups (median split) within each country
  • 2×2 ANOVA: Country × Awareness → ES priorities
  • Test interaction: Does disaster awareness effect differ by country?
  • Expected pattern: Awareness increases ALL ES in both countries, but differential effects (extractive up more in Poland, intrinsic up more in Germany)

Step 5: Within-Country Heterogeneity

Test whether between-country variance exceeds within-country variance:

  • Variance decomposition: How much total variance in ES priorities is explained by country vs. individual characteristics?
  • If within-country variance >> between-country variance, this suggests individual differences (angler vs. non-angler, urban vs. rural) matter more than national identity
  • If between-country variance is substantial even after controls, this confirms genuine cultural divergence

Step 6: Governance & Trust Analysis

Section E provides stakeholder trust ratings and preferred involvement. Test:

  • German-Polish differences in trust (government, scientists, NGOs, industry)
  • German-Polish differences in who should be involved in management
  • Mediation model: Country → Trust → Regulatory ES valuation
  • Logic: If you don't trust institutions, you don't support ES that require institutional action (flood control, climate regulation)

Step 7: Angler Subsample (Bonus Analysis, Data-Dependent)

IF Polish catch log data available:

  • Identify anglers in ES survey, compare stated extractive ES priorities to revealed fishing behavior
  • Do Polish anglers who rate fishing ES highly also fish more frequently/harvest more?
  • Do German anglers show weaker stated-revealed alignment because wild fishing is less available?
  • This would connect Papers 1-3 (revealed behavior) to Paper 5 (stated priorities)

Step 8: Sensitivity Analyses

  • Alternative ES factor structures (test whether factor structure differs by country = measurement invariance)
  • Restrict to border regions only (Brandenburg in Germany, Lubuskie in Poland) vs. include whole countries
  • Exclude anglers to test whether angler/non-angler divide obscures country effects
  • Test whether German-Polish differences are linear or if Poland is outlier (compare to other European countries' ES priorities from literature)

Expected Results

Scenario A: Extractive Gap, Intrinsic Convergence

Poles rate fishing/economic ES 0.8-1.2 points higher than Germans (on 5-point scale), effect size d=0.6-0.8. This is the largest cross-border gap. Both nations rate water quality, flood, biodiversity highly (means 4.2-4.5), with Germans 0.2-0.3 points higher on intrinsic ES. Decomposition shows 45% of extractive ES gap is explained by demographics/values, 55% is unexplained "cultural" component. Implication: Germans and Poles fundamentally conceptualize river benefits differently—Poles see resource for extraction, Germans see intrinsic value to protect.

Scenario B: Disaster Amplifies Differences

High disaster awareness increases all ES valuation by 0.3-0.5 points in both countries. But significant Country × Awareness interaction: Awareness increases extractive ES more in Poland (+0.6 points) than Germany (+0.2 points); increases intrinsic ES more in Germany (+0.5) than Poland (+0.3). Cross-border gap is 30% larger among high-awareness respondents. Implication: Same disaster, different lessons learned—Poles focus on economic losses, Germans on ecological losses.

Scenario C: Trust Mediates Intrinsic Values

Germans trust government and scientists 0.8-1.0 points more than Poles. Mediation analysis shows trust partially mediates Country → Intrinsic ES relationship (indirect effect 25-30% of total). Implication: Poles may value biodiversity/regulation less because they distrust institutions responsible for delivering these services.

Scenario D: Budget Allocation Reveals "True" Priorities

In importance ratings, most ES rated 3.5-4.5 (compressed range). In budget allocation, clear priorities emerge: Germans allocate 35% to water/flood, 25% to biodiversity, 15% to aesthetics, 10% to fishing. Poles allocate 30% to water/flood, 20% to biodiversity, 20% to fishing/economic, 15% to aesthetics. Cross-border differences 2-3× larger in budget than importance ratings. Implication: When forced to choose, national differences become stark.

Discussion

Theoretical Contributions

1. Cultural Contingency of ES Valuation

Confirms that ES perception is culturally contingent, not universal. Even with identical ecological conditions, national contexts shape which services are recognized, valued, and prioritized. This challenges universalist assumptions in ES framework—we cannot simply "map ES" and expect everyone to agree on priorities.

2. Co-Production Framework

Demonstrates three-way interaction among ecology, economy, and culture in shaping ES priorities. Ecological conditions matter (Poles can fish, Germans can't), but don't fully determine priorities. Economic development shifts priorities (post-industrial aesthetics vs. development economics), but cultural-institutional factors remain even after controlling for wealth. Culture operates independently.

3. Disaster as Natural Experiment

Environmental crises affect ES valuation, but not uniformly—disaster interpretation is culturally mediated. Same event, different meanings: economic loss vs. biodiversity tragedy. This has implications for post-disaster communication and recovery planning.

4. Stated Preferences in Cross-Cultural Context

Shows that stated preference methods (importance ratings, budget allocation) can detect cross-cultural differences reliably, but elicitation method matters—forced tradeoffs reveal priorities more clearly than importance ratings where everything is "important."

Management Implications

For Oder River Transboundary Management:

  • Build on shared ground: Water quality and flood protection have universal support—emphasize these as common goals to establish cooperation foundation
  • Acknowledge divergent priorities: Don't force false consensus. Germans prioritize biodiversity/aesthetics, Poles prioritize fishing/economics. Management strategies must accommodate both, not pretend conflict doesn't exist
  • Avoid zero-sum framing: Present restoration as win-win: healthy ecosystems support BOTH intrinsic values (German priority) AND sustainable fisheries (Polish priority). Economic-ecology tradeoff is false dichotomy in long run
  • Tailor communication: German messaging emphasize biodiversity recovery and future generations. Polish messaging emphasize fishery restoration and economic benefits. Same actions, different rationales
  • Address trust deficit: Polish stakeholder skepticism of institutions must be acknowledged. Participatory processes, transparent decision-making, and Polish institutional leadership can build trust
  • Leverage anglers as bridge: Anglers in both countries value both biodiversity AND fishing. Angling community may be key constituency that bridges German-Polish divide, as they appreciate ecosystem health for utilitarian reasons

For Broader Transboundary Governance:

  • Avoid assuming shared ecological conditions create shared values
  • Conduct cross-border ES assessments BEFORE designing management strategies
  • Use multiple elicitation methods—importance ratings alone underestimate divergence
  • Recognize that environmental crises can amplify existing value differences rather than creating unity
  • Build institutional trust as prerequisite for support of regulatory ES

Limitations

  • Translation equivalence: Despite professional translation, some ES concepts may have different connotations in German vs. Polish. "Sense of place" might not translate perfectly. Factor analysis testing measurement invariance addresses this partially.
  • Causal complexity: Cannot fully isolate "culture" from "ecology" from "economics"—all confounded with country. Can describe differences and test mediators, but explaining WHY differences exist requires interpretive leaps.
  • Selection into proximity: People living near degraded rivers may be less environmentally oriented (moved there because land was cheap). Proximity effects could confound country effects.
  • Temporal changes: Cross-sectional snapshot 3 years post-disaster. Can't assess whether German-Polish differences existed pre-disaster or emerged due to differential disaster experiences. Historical baseline would strengthen causal inference.
  • Generalizability: Oder case is specific—highly industrialized border river, major disaster, EU context with development gap. Findings may not transfer to other transboundary rivers with different characteristics.
  • Stated preferences only: Without Polish catch log data, cannot validate whether stated priority differences predict behavioral differences. Germans and Poles might talk differently but behave similarly, or vice versa.

Future Research

  • Longitudinal tracking: Repeat survey in 5 years to test whether cross-border differences narrow (convergence as recovery progresses) or widen (divergent recovery strategies reinforce different values)
  • Stated-revealed comparison: If Polish catch log data materialize, test whether stated ES priorities predict actual angler behavior. Do value differences translate to behavioral differences?
  • Comparative case studies: Apply same survey to other European transboundary rivers (Rhine, Danube, Elbe) to test generalizability. Are German-Polish differences unique or typical of Western-Eastern European borders?
  • Historical analysis: Reconstruct ES priorities pre-2022 using archival data, media analysis, or retrospective interviews. Did disaster change priorities or reveal latent differences?
  • Experimental interventions: Test whether emphasizing shared values (water quality, flood protection) in communication materials increases support for joint management among German and Polish residents
  • Institutional analysis: Deep dive into German-Polish management institutions. How do cross-border governance structures (or lack thereof) shape ES priorities and collaboration potential?
