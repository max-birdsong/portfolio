Adaptive Harvest Behavior: How Anglers Adjust Keep Decisions in Response to Declining Fish Populations

Abstract

Introduction & Background

Social-Ecological Feedbacks in Fisheries

Fisheries are classic social-ecological systems where human behavior (harvest) affects biological populations (fish stocks), which in turn influence human behavior (effort, satisfaction, harvest decisions). Most fisheries management research focuses on top-down regulation: setting size limits, bag limits, seasonal closures. However, voluntary restraint by anglers—choosing to release fish even when legal to keep—can significantly reduce harvest pressure and aid recovery (Bartholomew & Bohnsack 2005, Cooke & Cowx 2004).

The key question is whether voluntary conservation emerges adaptively in response to population declines, or whether anglers continue harvesting until prevented by regulations. Evidence is mixed. Some studies show anglers voluntarily reduce harvest when populations decline (self-regulation), while others show continued harvest until stocks collapse (Radomski et al. 2001, Hardin 1968 "tragedy of the commons").

Determinants of Catch-and-Release Behavior

Previous research identifies several predictors of voluntary catch-and-release:

Individual Characteristics:

  • Specialization: Highly specialized anglers practice more catch-and-release, valuing the challenge and sport over consumption (Graefe 1980, Arlinghaus et al. 2007). Empirical work in German consumptive angler culture (Birdsong et al., in preparation) confirms that specialization predicts lower harvest rates across multiple species, even in contexts where harvest is the traditional norm.
  • Conservation orientation: Environmental values and awareness of population status predict release behavior (Dunlap et al. 2000)
  • Experience: Veteran anglers more likely to release fish, possibly due to accumulated conservation norms (Fedler & Ditton 1994)

Fish Characteristics:

  • Species: Trophy species (pike, muskie, bass) released more often than panfish (perch, sunfish), reflecting different management cultures (Schramm et al. 1991)
  • Size: Very large fish often released (trophy status) and very small fish released (below quality threshold), with intermediate sizes most likely kept (Schill et al. 2010)
  • Catch rate: When CPUE is high, anglers more likely to release (abundance signal), but relationship is complex (Aas & Ditton 1998)

Social Context:

  • Club membership: Angling clubs often promote conservation norms through peer influence and education (Granek et al. 2008)
  • Regulations: Mandatory catch-and-release regulations normalize the behavior and can shift norms even when regulations end (Pierce & Tomberlin 2003)

The Oder River Context

The 2022 disaster creates a unique natural experiment for studying adaptive harvest behavior:

  1. Known catastrophic event: Unlike gradual population declines, anglers were acutely aware of the fish kill and its impacts
  2. No regulatory change: Size and bag limits remained constant before/after, so any harvest changes reflect voluntary decisions
  3. Uniform shock: All anglers experienced the same ecological decline simultaneously, controlling for differential awareness
  4. Multiple years of data: Can observe whether restraint is immediate, gradual, or nonexistent
  5. Consumptive culture context: German angling culture traditionally emphasizes harvest and consumption (Arlinghaus et al. 2007), making voluntary restraint particularly noteworthy. Previous research (Birdsong et al., in preparation) documented baseline harvest patterns in this context, providing comparison points for post-disaster behavior.

Research Gap

While many studies examine catch-and-release in established trophy fisheries or under experimental manipulation, few document spontaneous emergence of conservation behavior following ecological disasters. Previous work examining voluntary release behavior in German consumptive angler culture (Birdsong et al., in preparation) established baseline patterns across species and angler types under normal conditions. However, whether conservation ethics strengthen, weaken, or remain stable during ecological crises—when the conservation stakes are highest—remains an open question.

Most disaster research focuses on economic impacts (lost revenue, tourism declines) rather than behavioral adaptation. The Oder case allows us to test whether conservation ethics emerge bottom-up during crises or require top-down regulatory intervention. This extends previous harvest behavior research by examining adaptive responses to acute ecological stress rather than cross-sectional patterns in stable systems.

Research Questions

  1. RQ1: How do harvest rates (proportion of catch kept) change from 2023-2025?
  2. RQ2: Do anglers voluntarily practice more catch-and-release as populations recover slowly, or does harvest remain constant?
  3. RQ3: Which factors predict keep vs. release decisions at the individual fish level (species, size, angler characteristics, trip context)?
  4. RQ4: Does harvest restraint correlate with conservation-oriented characteristics (specialization, club membership) suggesting norm-driven behavior?
  5. RQ5: Is voluntary restraint sufficient to aid recovery, or do current harvest levels still impede biological restoration?

Hypotheses

H1: Temporal Trend in Harvest

H1a: Harvest rates will decline from 2023 to 2025, indicating adaptive voluntary restraint as anglers recognize population impacts.

H1b: The decline will be steeper in 2023-2024 (immediate response) than 2024-2025 (plateauing as new norm established).

Competing H1c: Alternatively, harvest rates may remain stable if anglers believe kept fish are "wasted" otherwise or if subsistence motivations dominate.

H2: Fish Characteristics

H2a: Trophy species (pike, zander, catfish) will have lower harvest rates than panfish/coarse fish (perch, roach, bream), reflecting established management cultures.

H2b: Larger fish (within legal limits) will have lower harvest rates than medium-sized fish, following the U-shaped size-selectivity pattern documented in other systems.

H2c: When trip CPUE is high (multiple fish caught), harvest rate per fish will be lower, as anglers are selective. When CPUE is low (few fish), harvest rate will be higher (keep what you get).

H3: Angler Characteristics

H3a: Highly specialized anglers (high specialization index) will show lower harvest rates than casual anglers, reflecting conservation-oriented values.

H3b: Club members will have lower harvest rates than non-members, reflecting social norm influence and club conservation messaging.

H3c: Specialization and club membership effects will be stronger in later years (2024-2025) as conservation norms diffuse and strengthen post-disaster.

H4: Trip Context

H4a: Target species affects harvest: trips targeting trophy species will have lower overall harvest rates.

H4b: Method matters: spin fishing (active, sport-oriented) will show lower harvest rates than still fishing (passive, potentially subsistence-oriented).

H4c: Season affects harvest: spring/summer harvest rates will be lower than fall (pre-winter stocking up) or winter (consumption-focused).

H5: Regulatory Compliance vs. Voluntary Restraint

H5: Observed harvest rates will be significantly below bag limits (e.g., if limit is 5 fish/day, average kept will be <2), indicating voluntary restraint beyond regulatory requirements. If harvest rates approach limits, this suggests regulations are binding rather than voluntary conservation driving behavior.

Data Requirements

Primary Data: Individual Fish Records

Fish-Level Data (Unit of Analysis)

Each row = one fish caught

Outcome variable:

  • Kept (1) or Released (0) — binary outcome

Fish characteristics:

  • Species (pike, zander, perch, bream, roach, carp, catfish, eel, other)
  • Length (cm) — recorded if ≥20cm and kept; missing for released fish in most cases
  • Sequence: fish #1, #2, #3... within trip (did they keep first fish and release later ones?)
Trip-Level Context (Nested)

Characteristics of the fishing trip:

  • Total fish caught (trip CPUE context)
  • Target species
  • Method (spin, still, fly)
  • Season (spring, summer, fall, winter)
  • Year (2023, 2024, 2025)
  • Location (river section/km)
  • Hours fished
Angler-Level Characteristics (Nested)

Stable individual traits:

  • Specialization index (see Paper 1)
  • Club membership (yes/no)
  • Years fishing (experience)
  • Annual days fishing

Sample Requirements

Minimum: 5,000 fish records from 500+ trips by 100+ anglers

Ideal: 20,000+ fish records from 2,000+ trips by 300+ anglers, enabling:

  • Sufficient power for three-level models (fish within trips within anglers)
  • Species-specific analyses (some species rare)
  • Temporal trend detection (need adequate representation across years)
  • Club member subsample analysis

Analytical Approach

Step 1: Descriptive Temporal Trends

Calculate aggregate harvest rates by:

  • Year (2023, 2024, 2025)
  • Species
  • Specialization tertile
  • Club membership
  • Season

Visualize trends with 95% confidence intervals to assess whether changes are statistically meaningful or within noise.

Step 2: Three-Level Logistic Regression

Model Structure:

Level 1 (Fish): Keep/release decision for individual fish

Level 2 (Trips): Fishing trips, capturing trip-specific factors

Level 3 (Anglers): Individual anglers, capturing stable person traits

Base Model:

Outcome: P(Fish kept = 1)

Level 1 predictors (fish): Species, length (if available), sequence within trip

Level 2 predictors (trip): Year, season, CPUE, target species, method

Level 3 predictors (angler): Specialization, club membership, experience

Random effects: (1 | Angler ID / Trip ID)

Interaction Models:

Test key interactions:

  • Year × Specialization: Do specialized anglers increase restraint more over time? (H3c)
  • Year × Club membership: Do club norms strengthen post-disaster? (H3c)
  • CPUE × Specialization: Are specialized anglers more selective when catch rates improve?
  • Species × Year: Does restraint increase more for trophy species vs. panfish?

Step 3: Species-Specific Models

Run separate models for major species groups:

  • Pike: Trophy species, expected low harvest
  • Perch: Panfish, expected high harvest
  • Zander: Trophy/table fare, intermediate harvest

Compare whether temporal trends (Year coefficient) differ across species, testing whether conservation norms are species-specific.

Step 4: Voluntary Restraint Assessment

For each angler, calculate:

  • Proportion of trips approaching bag limit (e.g., >80% of limit)
  • Distribution of fish kept per trip relative to limit
  • Whether any anglers consistently max out harvest (regulation-constrained) vs. most stay well below (voluntary restraint)

If >50% of anglers regularly approach limits, regulations are binding. If <20% approach limits, voluntary conservation is dominant.

Step 5: Counterfactual Comparison

If pre-2022 historical data exist (even informal club records), compare:

  • Pre-disaster harvest rates (e.g., 2019-2021 average)
  • Post-disaster harvest rates (2023-2025 average)
  • Calculate effect size: "Harvest declined by X percentage points post-disaster"

Without historical data, compare to:

  • Other German rivers (regional norms)
  • Published harvest rate benchmarks for similar systems

Step 6: Sensitivity Analyses

  • Alternative model specifications (logistic vs. probit)
  • Subset to high-frequency anglers (≥10 trips/year) to test if patterns hold among most active
  • Exclude rare species (<100 observations) to ensure robust estimates
  • Test nonlinear time trends (quadratic year term) to see if restraint plateaus
  • Control for trip success (caught ≥1 fish) since can't harvest if didn't catch

Expected Results & Interpretation

Scenario A: Voluntary Conservation Emerges

If H1a-b supported and H3a-c supported, we find declining harvest rates over time, especially among specialized anglers and club members. This indicates bottom-up emergence of conservation norms following the disaster. Interpretation: Anglers recognize population impacts and self-regulate. Management implication: Rely on voluntary conservation, focus on education and norm reinforcement rather than restrictive regulations.

Scenario B: Stable Harvest (No Adaptation)

If harvest rates remain constant 2023-2025, this suggests anglers are not voluntarily restraining harvest despite population declines. Could indicate: (1) subsistence motivations override conservation concerns, (2) lack of awareness about recovery needs, or (3) tragedy of the commons—individual incentive to harvest persists despite collective harm. Management implication: Voluntary conservation insufficient, consider tighter regulations or education campaigns.

Scenario C: Species-Specific Patterns

We may find restraint for trophy species (pike/zander already have catch-and-release culture) but continued high harvest of panfish (perch/roach viewed as abundant/renewable). This would indicate norms are culturally embedded and species-specific rather than responding to overall ecosystem health. Management implication: Target conservation messaging to panfish harvest, potentially introduce trip limits.

Scenario D: Social Norm Effects

If club membership strongly predicts lower harvest and this effect strengthens over time, this demonstrates social norm diffusion. Clubs may be actively promoting conservation messaging post-disaster. Management implication: Partner with angling clubs as conservation ambassadors, leverage peer influence for behavior change.

Scenario E: Regulation-Constrained Behavior

If many anglers consistently approach bag limits, this suggests regulations are binding and any restraint is forced rather than voluntary. Management would already be at the limit of what regulations can achieve without further tightening limits.

Discussion Points

Theoretical Contributions

  • Social-ecological feedback: Documents whether human behavior adapts to ecological conditions without regulatory intervention
  • Norm emergence: Tests whether conservation ethics emerge spontaneously during crises or require institutional support
  • Commons dilemma: Examines whether anglers overcome collective action problems when ecological stakes are clear
  • Specialization theory: Clarifies whether specialized recreationists lead conservation behavior change. Previous work (Birdsong et al., in preparation) established that specialization predicts catch-and-release in stable systems; this tests whether the relationship holds or strengthens under ecological stress.
  • Context-dependent behavior: Extends understanding of how social-ecological context moderates angler behavior (Birdsong et al. 2022), examining whether crisis conditions fundamentally alter behavioral patterns or merely amplify existing individual differences.

Management Implications

  • Adaptive management: If voluntary restraint is occurring, managers can use catch logs as leading indicators of angler conservation ethic and adjust regulations dynamically
  • Education vs. regulation tradeoff: Evidence of voluntary restraint suggests education and norm-based approaches may be effective, avoiding restrictive regulations that reduce satisfaction
  • Club partnerships: If club members show greater restraint, clubs are effective conservation partners
  • Species-specific messaging: If restraint varies by species, target education to species where harvest remains high
  • Monitoring tool: Harvest rates can serve as social indicator of fishery health alongside biological monitoring

Limitations

  • Self-reported data: Anglers may over-report release behavior (social desirability bias). Validation through creel surveys or harvest monitoring would strengthen findings.
  • Selection bias: Only anglers who continued fishing post-disaster are observed. Those most opposed to harvest may have quit entirely (not captured).
  • Causality: Correlation between specialization and release behavior doesn't prove specialization causes conservation ethic. Could be reverse causation or third variables.
  • Length data limitations: Size-selectivity analysis limited if lengths only recorded for kept fish. Released fish length rarely measured in citizen science.
  • Regulation knowledge: We need to verify regulations didn't change post-2022. If limits tightened, observed restraint may be forced compliance not voluntary conservation.

Future Research Directions

  • Stated vs. revealed conservation: Compare catch log harvest behavior to stated conservation attitudes in resident ES survey
  • Cross-border comparison: If Polish catch log data become available, test whether conservation norms differ culturally
  • Long-term tracking: Continue monitoring as populations recover—does harvest increase when ecological pressure eases?
  • Experimental nudges: Test whether messaging interventions (e.g., "95% of anglers release pike") increase restraint
  • Economic valuation: Estimate forgone harvest value to quantify voluntary conservation contribution to recovery

Policy Recommendations (Data-Dependent)

If voluntary restraint is strong:

  • Maintain current regulations, avoid tightening
  • Publicly acknowledge angler conservation contributions
  • Partner with clubs to reinforce conservation norms
  • Monitor trends to ensure restraint persists

If restraint is weak or absent:

  • Consider temporary harvest restrictions (e.g., 3-year recovery period)
  • Implement mandatory catch-and-release for key species
  • Launch education campaigns on population status
  • Increase enforcement presence to ensure compliance

If patterns are species-specific:

  • Targeted regulations for high-harvest species only
  • Celebrate success (e.g., "pike anglers show 90% release rate!")
  • Species-specific messaging for panfish harvest
